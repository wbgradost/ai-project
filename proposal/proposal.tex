\section{The question and the track}

Can AI assistance raise long-run skill when it not only substitutes for human
effort in current production, but also makes the remaining effort more
effective at producing skill? I study this question under \textbf{Track A} by
changing one assumption in the endogenous-skill model of
\citet{aouad2026paradoxes}. Their Proposition~3.5 (p.~8) establishes a weak
downward shift of the stationary skill distribution as AI assistance rises. My
conjecture is deliberately narrower: sufficiently strong AI--effort
complementarity in learning reverses that comparison over an initial range of
assistance, but effort displacement eventually dominates. The objective is an
exact two-state characterization, not a universal claim that more AI always
improves skill.

\section{The baseline and the assumption I change}

\paragraph{Worker problem.}
A myopic worker has skill $s>0$, chooses effort $e\geq 0$, and receives AI
assistance $a\geq0$. Skill, effort, and AI are perfect substitutes in current
production. With an increasing, concave production function $p$ and linear
effort cost $\gamma e$, $\gamma>0$, the worker solves
\begin{equation}
  e^*(s,a)\in\arg\max_{e\geq0}
  \left\{p(s+e+a)-\gamma e\right\}.                 \label{eq:worker}
\end{equation}
The Kuhn--Tucker conditions are
\begin{equation}
 e^*\geq0,\qquad
 \gamma-p'(s+e^*+a)\geq0,\qquad
 e^*\bigl[\gamma-p'(s+e^*+a)\bigr]=0.              \label{eq:kkt}
\end{equation}
Let $x^*$ be the largest maximizer of $p(x)-\gamma x$. Under the paper's
boundedness condition, Proposition~2.1 gives
\begin{equation}
 e^*(s,a)=(x^*-s-a)^+ .                              \label{eq:effort}
\end{equation}
Thus, while effort is positive, an additional unit of AI displaces one unit of
effort.

\paragraph{Baseline skill dynamics.}
The original model is a continuous-time birth--death chain. From state $s_i$,
the upward rate is $\lambda(e^*(s_i,a))$, where $\lambda$ is positive,
increasing, differentiable, and weakly concave; the downward rate is the
constant $\mu>0$. Local balance gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\pi_{i+1}(a)}{\pi_i(a)}
 =\frac{\lambda(e^*(s_i,a))}{\mu}.                  \label{eq:odds}
\end{equation}
Because (3) is decreasing in $a$ and the baseline $\lambda$ is increasing in
effort, every adjacent stationary odds ratio falls with AI. Proposition~3.5
therefore shows that lower assistance first-order stochastically dominates
higher assistance. Its Appendix~A.4 proves this result by comparing normalized
sums of products of the ratios in (4).

\paragraph{Changed assumption.}
I preserve (1), myopia, current production, and the downward rate, but replace
the effort-only learning technology with
\begin{equation}
 \lambda(e,a)=\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a),
 \qquad \lambda_0>0,\ \kappa>0,\ \eta\geq0.         \label{eq:learning}
\end{equation}
For each fixed $a$, (5) is positive, increasing, and linear (hence weakly
concave) in effort. Its cross-partial is $\lambda_{ea}=\kappa\eta\geq0$: AI
raises the learning effectiveness of effort. When $\eta=0$, (5) is an affine
special case of the baseline. Since the worker remains myopic, (5) does not
change the first-order conditions (2); it changes only the transition rate
evaluated at optimal effort.

\section{The result I expect}

Consider two skill states $s_L<s_H$. Write
$\lambda_L(a)=\lambda(e^*(s_L,a),a)$. Stationary flow balance and normalization
are
\begin{equation}
 \pi_L\lambda_L=\pi_H\mu,\qquad \pi_L+\pi_H=1,
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \pi_H(a)=\frac{\lambda_L(a)}{\lambda_L(a)+\mu}.     \label{eq:stationary}
\end{equation}
Let $b=x^*-s_L>0$. By (3) and (5),
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_L(a)=\lambda_0+\kappa(b-a)^+(1+\eta a).    \label{eq:equilibrium-rate}
\end{equation}
In the active-effort region $0\leq a<b$,
\begin{align}
 \lambda_L(a)
   &=\lambda_0+\kappa\bigl[b+(\eta b-1)a-\eta a^2\bigr],\notag\\
 \frac{d\lambda_L}{da}
   &=\underbrace{\kappa\eta(b-a)}_{\text{learning complementarity}}
     -\underbrace{\kappa(1+\eta a)}_{\text{effort crowd-out}}\notag\\
   &=\kappa\bigl[\eta(b-2a)-1\bigr],\label{eq:dlambda}\\
 \frac{d\pi_H}{da}
   &=\frac{\mu\kappa\,[\eta(b-2a)-1]}
           {[\lambda_L(a)+\mu]^2}.                 \label{eq:dpi}
\end{align}
The positive term in \eqref{eq:dlambda} is the direct increase in learning effectiveness;
the negative term is the loss caused by effort crowd-out. This yields the
candidate proposition. If $\eta b\leq1$, the high-skill share is weakly
decreasing, as in the baseline. If $\eta b>1$, it increases up to
\begin{equation}
 a^\dagger=\frac{\eta b-1}{2\eta},                  \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
then decreases for $a^\dagger<a<b$. At the second threshold $a=b$, effort
reaches zero; thereafter $\lambda_L=\lambda_0$ and
$\pi_H=\lambda_0/(\lambda_0+\mu)$ is constant. For any
$0\leq a_\ell<a_h<b$, the exact pairwise condition is
\begin{equation}
 \pi_H(a_h)>\pi_H(a_\ell)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \eta\bigl(b-a_h-a_\ell\bigr)>1.                  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:pair} is the proposed local reversal of Proposition~3.5. It also makes
the limitation explicit: if the higher assistance level crosses $b$, the
complementarity term disappears and cannot sustain a global reversal.

\section{Why this is not already solved}

I reviewed the current arXiv version of \citet{aouad2026paradoxes}, including
Appendix~A.4 and the convex-cost extension in Appendix~D. Their skill
transition remains a function of effort alone; the appendices do not introduce
AI into the learning technology or derive \eqref{eq:dlambda}--\eqref{eq:pair}. ArXiv currently lists
only version~1; the authors also identify an EC~2026 conference version.

This project does \emph{not} claim that AI--learning complementarity is new.
Most directly, \citet{davies2026course} studies a myopic student who may work
or delegate to AI. His Theorem~4 lets AI quality raise the marginal product of
effort and shows that higher quality can increase learning for high-skill
students while reducing it for low-skill students. That model endogenizes
course design and discrete delegation, whereas this project keeps the worker
problem and stationary birth--death structure of \citet{aouad2026paradoxes}
and puts complementarity directly in the transition rate. The added result is
the closed-form stationary reversal condition and its assistance threshold.
Recent experiments also distinguish productive AI use from effort-replacing
use \citep{shen2026skill,wu2026assistance}; they motivate the mechanism but do
not provide this stationary comparative static.

\section{Plan and risks}

\textbf{Proof.} I will prove the two-state proposition with all boundary and
equality cases, then determine whether state-by-state transition inequalities
yield a useful $N$-state stochastic-dominance result. \textbf{Verification.}
After the proposition is fixed, a symbolic script will verify
\eqref{eq:stationary}--\eqref{eq:pair}, and a
parameter-grid simulation will test sign changes, threshold crossings, and
limiting cases; these are checks, not proofs. \textbf{Lean.} I will formalize
the two-state stationary identity, the sign equivalence in \eqref{eq:dpi}, and the
pairwise condition \eqref{eq:pair}, including positivity assumptions and the piecewise
effort boundary. These components are intentionally deferred until the
economic statement has been reviewed.

The main mathematical risk is that the clean two-state ordering need not
extend to $N$ states when different effort thresholds are crossed. The fallback
is to state exact sufficient conditions on every upward rate rather than claim
a global reversal. The main economic risks are the maintained myopia assumption
and the fact that, in two states, high-state effort affects output but not
further learning. Finally, (5) is concave in effort for fixed AI but not jointly
concave in $(e,a)$; the paper will state the required domain-specific
assumption explicitly.
