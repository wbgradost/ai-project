\begin{abstract}
\noindent Can AI assistance improve long-run skill while displacing human
effort? I retain the myopic worker problem of Aouad, Lykouris, and Zhong
(2026) and introduce AI--effort complementarity in learning. In a two-state
continuous-time Markov chain with low-state effort $b>0$ without AI, the
stationary high-skill share initially increases exactly when complementarity
$\eta$ satisfies $\eta b>1$. The share peaks at $(b-1/\eta)/2$ and returns to
its no-AI value at $b-1/\eta$. Effort vanishes at $b$, after which the share
is constant. Exact pairwise comparisons characterize when one assistance
level improves on another. Formula evaluations and Markov simulations check
the results, with Monte Carlo error measured separately from
initial-condition bias. The Lean development proves the five numbered results. Appendix~ef{app:lean}
records the declarations and the scope of verification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
AI assistance can save effort on today's task while changing what a worker
learns from doing it. If acquiring skill requires practice, the effort saved
today can reduce the chance of becoming more skilled tomorrow. Assistance
can also make the practice that remains more productive, for example by
providing feedback while a worker still solves the problem. When can this
learning effect raise long-run skill despite the displacement of effort?

The starting point is Proposition~3.5 of \citet[p.~8]{aouad2026paradoxes}.
In their model, an increase in assistance weakly reduces effort and lowers
the stationary skill distribution in the weak first-order stochastic
order. I specialize the model to two skill states, retain its additive
production technology, myopic effort decision and constant depreciation
rate, and change the upward transition rate. Assistance raises the
learning productivity of each unit of effort. The worker still chooses
effort using the same current payoff and the same selection rule.

The product $\eta b$, where $\eta$ measures learning complementarity and
$b$ is low-state effort without AI, determines whether a small increase
from zero raises stationary skill. When $\eta b>1$, the stationary
high-skill share initially rises, reaches a unique maximum $a^*$, and
returns to its no-AI level at $a_R=2a^*$. Between these thresholds, more
assistance lowers the high-skill share, although the share remains above
its no-AI value. The exact comparison of any two assistance levels,
including levels at which effort is zero, determines which stationary
skill distribution dominates. The resulting thresholds identify how far
the original stationary ordering can reverse while the worker's effort
continues to decline.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
The baseline is \citet[Proposition~3.5, p.~8, and Appendix~A.4]{aouad2026paradoxes},
the May 2026 arXiv version. Its endogenous-skill model has $N$ ordered
skill states, a concave production function with additive inputs, linear
effort cost and myopic effort choice. Between adjacent states in the
birth--death chain, the upward rate is $\lambda(e^*(s_i,a))$, where
$\lambda$ is positive, increasing and weakly concave, and the downward
rate is a constant $\mu>0$. Proposition~2.1 gives a weakly decreasing
effort response to assistance. Hence every upward-to-downward rate ratio
weakly decreases with assistance. For each cutoff $s_k$, the stationary
cumulative probability is weakly larger at higher assistance. The order
is weak, so equal stationary distributions are possible. This is a
skill-distribution result. The paper's productivity analysis also
compares output within each skill state.

The October revision gives the corresponding stochastic order in
\citet[Proposition~3.3, p.~10, and Appendix~B.3]{aouad2026paradoxesv2}.
Its production specification allows $p(e+h(s,a))$ with $h$ increasing
in assistance, and the ratio argument continues to rely on learning
through effort alone. The authors list the paper as a working paper with
a conference version accepted at \href{https://ec26.sigecom.org/program/accepted-papers/}
{EC 2026}. The present extension uses
the May additive specification and allows the upward rate to respond
directly to assistance. In two states, the single rate ratio characterizes
the full stationary ordering, including the conditions for equality and
for reversal.

\citet{davies2026course}, a September 2026 working paper, studies a teacher's
choice of a task sequence when a myopic student can work or delegate to AI.
Under his complementarity conditions, higher AI quality can have different
effects on learning for students at different skill levels. His mechanism
provides a related example of complementarity in learning. Here the
assistance level is fixed, and the object is the stationary skill
distribution. In experiments on programming and logic puzzles,
\citet{shen2026skill} and \citet{wu2026assistance} distinguish assistance
that preserves engagement from assistance that displaces it. These
studies motivate the two channels in the model. The experiments measure
learning in specific tasks, whereas the model compares stationary
distributions under sustained assistance.

\section{Economic model}\label{sec:model}
Skill, effort, and assistance enter production in the same units.
Assistance $a$ is set exogenously and held fixed for the entire skill
trajectory. At any time $t\geq0$, the worker observes the current skill
state $X_t$ and chooses effort for that state. The transition rates then
reflect the chosen effort. The worker's objective includes current payoff
only.

\begin{assumption}[Worker problem and selection]\label{ass:worker}
The production function $p:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is continuously
differentiable, nondecreasing, and concave, and the marginal cost of effort
is constant at $\gamma>0$. Assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growth}
 \limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{p(x)}{x}<\gamma.
\end{equation}
For skill $s\geq0$ and assistance $a\geq0$, the worker selects the largest
maximizer of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:worker}
 \max_{e\geq0}\{p(s+e+a)-\gamma e\}.
\end{equation}
Let $x^*$ denote the largest maximizer of $p(x)-\gamma x$ over $x\geq0$.
\end{assumption}

Additive input makes effort and assistance perfect substitutes in
production. The upper selection rule specifies behavior if the utility
function has a flat maximum. Lemma~\ref{lem:effort} establishes existence
and characterizes both the interior choice and the zero-effort corner.

\begin{assumption}[States, learning, and depreciation]\label{ass:chain}
There are two skill states, $L$ and $H$, with skill levels
$0\leq s_L<s_H$. Define $b=x^*-s_L$ and assume $b>0$. For each fixed
$a\geq0$, $(X_t)_{t\geq0}$ is a time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov
chain on $\{L,H\}$. The rate from $L$ to $H$ at the chosen low-state effort
is given by the learning technology
\begin{equation}\label{eq:technology}
 \lambda(e,a)=\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a),\qquad
 e,a\geq0,\quad \lambda_0>0,\quad \kappa>0,\quad \eta\geq0.
\end{equation}
The rate from $H$ to $L$ is the constant $\mu>0$.
\end{assumption}

Baseline learning $\lambda_0$ persists at zero effort. The coefficient
$\kappa$ converts effective effort into a transition rate, and $\eta$
measures how much assistance increases the learning effectiveness of
effort. Since $1+\eta a$ is dimensionless, $\eta$ has inverse input units.
For fixed assistance, $\lambda$ is increasing and affine in effort, with
$\lambda_e=\kappa(1+\eta a)>0$ and $\lambda_{ee}=0$. The cross partial
$\lambda_{ea}=\kappa\eta$ measures complementarity in learning. Effort in
state $H$ affects production but has no upward transition to influence.

\begin{definition}[Effective effort]\label{def:f}
Write $y^+=\max\{y,0\}$ for the positive part of $y\in\mathbb R$. For
$a\geq0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
 f(a)=(b-a)^+(1+\eta a),\qquad \lam(a)=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a).
\end{equation}
Write $\share(a)$ and $\pi_L(a)$ for the stationary probabilities of $H$
and $L$, respectively.
\end{definition}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
The worker's rule determines the learning rate $\lam(a)$ faced in state
$L$. Positive upward and downward rates then give a unique stationary
distribution. The remaining results characterize how that distribution
varies with assistance. Proofs are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{lemma}[Effort and the largest-maximizer rule]\label{lem:effort}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:worker}, the largest maximizers defining $x^*$
and the worker's selected effort exist. For every $s,a\geq0$ the selected
effort is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effort}
 e^*(s,a)=(x^*-s-a)^+ .
\end{equation}
An interior choice satisfies $p'(x^*)=\gamma$. At the corner $s+a\geq x^*$,
the selected effort is zero and $p'(s+a)\leq\gamma$, strictly if
$s+a>x^*$. Under
Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}, $e_L^*(a)=b-a$ for $0\leq a<b$, with
derivative $-1$ on $(0,b)$, and $e_L^*(a)=0$ for $a\geq b$.
\end{lemma}

The objective in \eqref{eq:worker} contains no learning term, so the effort
schedule is the same for every $\eta$. In state $L$, each additional unit
of assistance displaces one unit of effort until assistance reaches $b$.
With \eqref{eq:effort}, the upward rate in Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}
equals $\lam(a)$ in \eqref{eq:f}.

\begin{lemma}[Stationarity and finite-horizon occupation]\label{lem:stationary}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, each fixed $a\geq0$
has a unique stationary distribution,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
 \share(a)=\frac{\lam(a)}{\lam(a)+\mu},\qquad
 \pi_L(a)=\frac{\mu}{\lam(a)+\mu}.
\end{equation}
Let $q_a=\lam(a)+\mu$ and let $h_0\in[0,1]$ be the initial probability
of state $H$. At time $t\geq0$, the probability of state $H$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transient}
 h(t)=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))e^{-q_at}.
\end{equation}
For $T>0$, the occupation fraction
$Y_T=T^{-1}\int_0^T\mathbf{1}_{\{X_t=H\}}\,dt$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occupation}
 \mathbb E[Y_T]=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))
                       \frac{1-e^{-q_aT}}{q_a T}.
\end{equation}
Starting in $L$ gives a downward bias relative to $\share(a)$ of
magnitude $\share(a)(1-e^{-q_aT})/(q_a T)$.
\end{lemma}

The map $g(\ell)=\ell/(\ell+\mu)$ has derivative
$g'(\ell)=\mu/(\ell+\mu)^2>0$ for $\ell>0$. Therefore the stationary share
inherits the ordering of the learning rate. Define the null regime as
$\eta=0$, the weak regime as $0<\eta b\leq1$, and the strong regime as
$\eta b>1$. The boundary $\eta b=1$ belongs to the weak regime.

\begin{proposition}[Global shape and the no-AI benchmark]\label{prop:shape}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, $\share$ is continuous
on $[0,\infty)$. For $0\leq a<b$, its derivative (from the right at $0$) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative}
 \share'(a)=\frac{\mu\kappa[\eta(b-2a)-1]}{[\lam(a)+\mu]^2}.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item In the null and weak regimes, $\share$ is strictly decreasing on
 $[0,b]$, has its unique global maximum at $a=0$, and satisfies
 $\share(a)<\share(0)$ for every $a>0$. At $\eta b=1$,
 $\share'(0+)=0$ and $\share'(a)<0$ for $0<a<b$.
 \item In the strong regime, define
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:thresholds}
  \peak=\frac{b-1/\eta}{2},\qquad \ret=b-1/\eta=2\peak .
 \end{equation}
 Then $0<\peak<\ret<b$ and $\peak<b/2$. The share is strictly increasing
 on $[0,\peak]$ and strictly decreasing on $[\peak,b]$. It reaches its
 unique global maximum at $\peak$. For positive assistance,
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline}
  \begin{aligned}
   \share(a)>\share(0)&\iff 0<a<\ret,\\
   \share(a)=\share(0)&\iff a=\ret,\\
   \share(a)<\share(0)&\iff a>\ret .
  \end{aligned}
 \end{equation}
 \item In every regime, for all $a\geq b$,
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:plateau}
  \lam(a)=\lambda_0,\qquad
  \share(a)=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_0+\mu}<\share(0).
 \end{equation}
 The one-sided derivatives at $b$ are
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:kink}
  \share'(b-)=-\frac{\mu\kappa(1+\eta b)}{(\lambda_0+\mu)^2}<0,\qquad
  \share'(b+)=0.
 \end{equation}
 The share is strictly concave on the active branch $[0,b]$ and fails
 global concavity on $[0,\infty)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

There is an interval of positive marginal effects exactly when $\eta b>1$.
The marginal effect changes sign at $\peak$. The gain relative to no AI
remains positive farther along the decreasing branch, up to $\ret$.
Assistance at or above $b$ fully displaces effort and leaves a common
stationary share across complementarity regimes.

\begin{proposition}[Comparison of two assistance levels]\label{prop:pairwise}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, let $0\leq u<v$
and let $D_w=\lambda_0+\kappa f(w)+\mu$ for $w\in\{u,v\}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:globaldiff}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=\frac{\mu\kappa[f(v)-f(u)]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
If $v\leq b$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairwise}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=
 \frac{\mu\kappa(v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
In this case $\share(v)$ is greater than, equal to, or less than $\share(u)$
exactly when $\eta(b-u-v)$ is greater than, equal to, or less than $1$,
respectively. If $u<b\leq v$, then $\share(v)<\share(u)$. If $b\leq u<v$,
then $\share(v)=\share(u)$. For two ordered states, $\share(v)\geq\share(u)$
is equivalent to first-order stochastic dominance of the stationary law
at $v$ over the law at $u$ and to at least as high a mean skill at $v$.
Strict inequalities in the shares give strict rankings in both comparisons.
\end{proposition}

In the strong regime an increase from $u$ to $v$ improves the stationary
skill distribution exactly when $u+v<\ret$. This can hold with $v>\peak$
if the starting assistance is sufficiently low. Active levels yield
equal shares precisely when $u+v=\ret$, so two distinct active levels with
equal shares lie on opposite sides of $\peak$.

\begin{corollary}[Threshold locations and outcome magnitudes]\label{cor:sensitivity}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, the critical
complementarity is $\eta_c=1/b$. In the strong regime, $\peak,\ret$ depend
only on $b,\eta$. Holding the other arguments fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-sensitivity}
 \partial_\eta\peak=\frac{1}{2\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_\eta\ret=\frac{1}{\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_b\peak=\frac12,\quad \partial_b\ret=1 .
\end{equation}
For fixed $a\geq0$ and $D=\lam(a)+\mu$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:level-sensitivity}
 \partial_{\lambda_0}\share=\frac{\mu}{D^2}>0,\qquad
 \partial_\kappa\share=\frac{\mu f(a)}{D^2}\geq0,\qquad
 \partial_\mu\share=-\frac{\lam(a)}{D^2}<0.
\end{equation}
Also $\partial_\eta\share=\mu\kappa a(b-a)^+/D^2\geq0$ (from the
right at $\eta=0$). The derivative in $\kappa$ is zero exactly when
$a\geq b$, and the derivative in $\eta$ is zero exactly when $a=0$ or
$a\geq b$. For $a<b$, $\partial_b\share=\mu\kappa(1+\eta a)/D^2>0$,
and for $a>b$ it is zero. At $b=a>0$ the left derivative in $b$ is zero
and the right derivative is $\mu\kappa(1+\eta a)/(\lambda_0+\mu)^2>0$.
Multiplying $(\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu)$ by a common factor $c>0$ leaves
$\share$ unchanged and multiplies the convergence rate $q_a$ by $c$.

For fixed $0<a<\ret$ in the strong regime, let $\Delta=f(a)-b>0$. The
positive stationary gain over no AI is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
 G(a)=\share(a)-\share(0)
 =\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta}{(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)+\mu)
                            (\lambda_0+\kappa b+\mu)}.
\end{equation}
It strictly decreases with $\lambda_0$. The derivative in $\mu$ has the
sign of $(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a))(\lambda_0+\kappa b)-\mu^2$, and the
derivative in $\kappa$ has the sign of
$(\lambda_0+\mu)^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b$, including zero at equality.
\end{corollary}

A larger $b$ lowers the complementarity required for reversal, since
$\partial_b\eta_c=-1/b^2$. In the strong regime, increasing either $b$ or
$\eta$ lengthens the interval of positive marginal effects and the
interval of gains relative to no AI. At fixed $b$, the thresholds
approach zero as $\eta$ decreases to $1/b$, and approach $b/2$ and $b$,
respectively, as $\eta$ tends to infinity. At fixed $b$, the peak stationary gain is of second order in the
distance $\eta b-1$ as $\eta$ approaches $1/b$ from above
(Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}).
The rate coefficients determine the magnitude of the stationary gain.
Larger $\kappa$ raises both active-state shares being compared, and
larger $\mu$ lowers both shares. Either change can increase or decrease
their difference. Holding other parameters fixed within the gain region,
the gain has a unique maximum as a function of each of these rates.
The derivative in $\mu$ vanishes at the geometric mean of the two
upward rates, while the derivative in $\kappa$ vanishes at
$(\lambda_0+\mu)/\sqrt{f(a)b}$.

The model's dimensionless inputs are $a/b$, $\eta b$, $\lambda_0/\mu$, and
$\kappa b/\mu$. The first two determine the shape comparisons after
normalizing assistance by the effort cutoff. The last two govern the
mapping from effective effort to a stationary probability.

\subsection{Continuous-time Markov simulations}
To check Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}'s predictions for time in $H$, each
path uses exact exponential holding times at the two exit rates. The
occupation fraction integrates all time in $H$, including any part of its
last holding spell before the horizon. The three values of $\eta$ are
crossed with $a\in\{\MCAssistanceGrid\}$ to form \MCCells{} cells covering
the benchmark, the active branch, and the plateau. Each cell uses
\MCReplications{} independent paths initialized in $L$, observed from
time zero, with child streams generated from seed \MCSeed. The same
trajectory supplies its occupation at $T\in\{\MCHorizons\}$.

Write $\widehat Y_T$ for the average occupation across paths in a cell.
Its difference from stationarity decomposes as
\[
 \widehat Y_T-\share(a)
  = \underbrace{\widehat Y_T-\mathbb E[Y_T]}_{\text{Monte Carlo error}}
   +\underbrace{\mathbb E[Y_T]-\share(a)}_{\text{initial-state bias}} .
\]
The second term is known from \eqref{eq:occupation}. The plotted nominal
$95\%$ intervals use a normal approximation. For each horizon, the
standard error is the sample standard deviation of path occupations
divided by the square root of the replication count. Estimates at
different horizons share paths.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Effort displacement and learning.}
For $0<a<b$, differentiating the learning rate along the worker's effort
choice gives $\lam'(a)=\kappa\eta(b-a)-\kappa(1+\eta a)$. The first term is
the gain from making each unit of remaining effort more effective.
It shrinks as the worker does less. The second term is the learning
lost from a unit reduction in effort, which grows in magnitude as
assistance makes that effort more valuable. Their balance explains both
the interior peak and the subsequent decline. At $a_R$, the loss in
learning accumulated since the peak offsets the earlier gain relative
to zero. Beyond $b$ there is no remaining effort for assistance to amplify.
With positive complementarity, a larger $b$ increases the scope for
assisted practice and lowers the required $\eta_c=1/b$. The numerical
exercises in Section~\ref{sec:sim} were motivated by feedback on the topic
presentation about the need to identify where these effects matter.

The critical condition depends on the linear displacement of effort and
the affine amplification of the learning it generates. The closed-form
thresholds, including $a_R=2a^*$, use the resulting quadratic. A different
response of learning effectiveness to assistance could change the shape
of the learning rate and the distance between these thresholds. Positive
unaided effort is essential here. When the no-AI effort is zero, the
positive-part rule leaves nothing for the complementarity to act on.
Likewise, holding depreciation fixed is what allows an increase in
learning to be read directly as an increase in the stationary high-skill
share.

\paragraph{Myopia and fixed assistance.}
Myopia isolates the transition technology from the worker's decision.
A worker who values future skill would also account for how present effort
changes the chance of moving to $H$. The learning return would then enter
the effort condition, so the unit displacement of effort and the reversal
threshold would require a new derivation. In the present model, each
comparison holds assistance constant for the entire evolution of skill.
It describes the stationary consequences of an assistance level maintained
over time. A policy that changes assistance as skill develops could expose
workers to different learning rates at different stages. Its performance
would depend on the policy and the horizon, in addition to the stationary
effects studied here.

\paragraph{Two skill states.}
The stationary distribution is determined by a single transition-rate
ratio. The level $s_H$ matters for mean skill and output even though it
does not enter the transition rates. With more states, no-AI effort and
its cutoff would differ across upward links of the chain. At the same
assistance level, one link's learning rate could rise while another's
falls. The stationary probabilities would then reflect a set of rate
ratios, and ordering every skill cutoff would require further conditions.

\paragraph{Skill, output, and welfare.}
The stationary share weights two skill levels. Stationary production
also depends on $p(s+e^*(s,a)+a)$ at each state, so assistance changes the
objects being weighted as well as their weights. For instance, for $a\ge b$
effort is zero in both states, and the skill share is constant. For
strictly increasing $p$, output in both states continues to rise with
assistance there. Indeed, a sufficiently large $a$ gives even the low
state a larger total input than either state has at $a^*$, and thereby
gives a higher stationary output despite a lower high-skill share.
The worker's current payoff additionally accounts for saved effort costs.
An assessment of welfare would require a criterion for valuing payoffs
over time and the resource cost of supplying assistance. The stationary
skill maximum answers how to sustain the most high skill within this
technology and this class of assistance levels.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In the two-state extension of Aouad, Lykouris, and Zhong, assistance
raises the stationary high-skill share over an initial interval if and
only if it makes the worker's remaining effort sufficiently productive
for learning, $\eta b>1$. The share then peaks at $a^*$ and remains above
its no-AI value until $a_R=2a^*$. The pairwise characterization identifies
exactly when more assistance raises, preserves, or lowers stationary
skill, including when effort has been entirely displaced. These results
show how a change in the learning technology can reverse the original
skill ordering with the same myopic incentives. They also identify the
limit of that reversal as assistance removes the practice on which the
learning gain depends.

\subsection{Effort and the largest-maximizer rule}\label{app:effort}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:effort}]
Put $z=s+a$ and $q(x)=p(x)-\gamma x$. By \eqref{eq:growth}, there are
$\varepsilon>0$ and $R>0$ such that $p(x)/x\leq\gamma-\varepsilon$
for all $x\geq R$. Thus $q(x)\leq-\varepsilon x$ for $x\geq R$, and
$q(x)\to-\infty$. For a sufficiently large $K$, every $x>K$ has
$q(x)<q(0)$. Continuity and the extreme value theorem on $[0,K]$
therefore give a nonempty compact set of global maximizers with largest
element $x^*$. Changing variables to $x=z+e$ rewrites \eqref{eq:worker}
as maximizing $q(x)+\gamma z$ over $x\geq z$. The change of variables
preserves the order of maximizers.

If $z\leq x^*$, then $x^*$ is feasible and is the largest constrained
maximizer. At $z=x^*$ it is the unique constrained maximizer, since every
larger $x$ has $q(x)<q(x^*)$. Now suppose $z>x^*$. For any $y>z$, concavity
orders the secant slopes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effort-secant}
 \frac{q(y)-q(z)}{y-z}\leq
 \frac{q(z)-q(x^*)}{z-x^*}<0 .
\end{equation}
The strict inequality follows because $z$ lies to the right of the
largest global maximizer. Consequently $z$ is the unique constrained
maximizer. Subtracting $z$ from the chosen total input
$\max\{x^*,z\}$ gives \eqref{eq:effort}.

When $z<x^*$, the selected choice has $e^*>0$, so its first-order
condition is $q'(x^*)=p'(x^*)-\gamma=0$. When $z\geq x^*$, $q$ is
maximized on $[z,\infty)$ at $z$, which gives $q'(z)\leq0$ by taking
the derivative from the right. When $z>x^*$, taking $y\downarrow z$
in \eqref{eq:effort-secant} shows that $q'(z)<0$. Finally substitute $s=s_L$ into
\eqref{eq:effort}. Since $x^*-s_L=b>0$, low-state effort is positive
with slope $-1$ before $b$ and is zero at and above $b$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stationary distribution and time in the high state}\label{app:stationary}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}]
Fix $a\geq0$ and write $\ell=\lam(a)$ and $q_a=\ell+\mu$.
Since $1+\eta a\geq1$, \eqref{eq:f} yields $f(a)\geq0$ and
$\ell\geq\lambda_0>0$. Also $\mu>0$, giving positive rates in both
directions. The finite state space and finite rates give a nonexplosive
chain. Its generator in the order $(L,H)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generator}
 Q(a)=\begin{pmatrix}-\ell & \ell\\ \mu & -\mu\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The equations for a normalized stationary law are
$\pi_L\ell=\share\mu$ and $\pi_L+\share=1$. Solving gives \eqref{eq:stationary}
with positive entries, since $q_a>0$. To see both that this law is
invariant and that no other initial law is invariant, consider the
probability of state $H$ at time $t+\delta$. By time homogeneity and the
generator, the respective probabilities of ending in $H$ over an interval
of length $\delta$ from $L$ and $H$ are $\ell\delta+o(\delta)$ and
$1-\mu\delta+o(\delta)$. Conditioning on $X_t$ therefore gives
\begin{align}
 h(t+\delta)
 &= (1-h(t))[\ell\delta+o(\delta)]
    +h(t)[1-\mu\delta+o(\delta)],\notag\\
 h'(t)&=\ell(1-h(t))-\mu h(t)=\ell-q_ah(t).
 \label{eq:forward}
\end{align}
The right derivative calculation extends to the ordinary derivative
for $t>0$ by continuity of the transition probabilities. In fact, the
same short-interval equation bounds $|h(t+\delta)-h(t)|$ by a constant
times $\delta$ for small $\delta>0$, and applying it at $t-\delta$ gives
the left derivative. Multiplying the centered equation by $e^{q_at}$
gives $(e^{q_at}(h(t)-\ell/q_a))'=0$, proving \eqref{eq:transient}.
The distribution with $h_0=\ell/q_a$ is invariant by this formula,
and any other $h_0$ gives a nonconstant $h(t)$.

In \eqref{eq:transient}, use $h_0=0$ for a starting state $L$ and
$h_0=1$ for $H$. Taking complementary probabilities for the first column
gives the transition matrix $P_a(t)$ with initial states indexing rows.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transition-matrix}
 P_a(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  \pi_L+\share e^{-q_at} & \share(1-e^{-q_at})\\
  \pi_L(1-e^{-q_at}) & \share+\pi_L e^{-q_at}
 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where both stationary shares are evaluated at $a$. Since the occupation
indicator is bounded and nonnegative, integration and expectation may
be interchanged. Specifically,
\begin{align*}
 T\,\mathbb E[Y_T]
 &=\int_0^T \mathbb E[\mathbf{1}_{\{X_t=H\}}]\,dt
   = \int_0^T h(t)\,dt\\
 &=T\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))\frac{1-e^{-q_aT}}{q_a}.
\end{align*}
Divide by $T$ to obtain \eqref{eq:occupation}. With $h_0=0$, the
difference $\mathbb E[Y_T]-\share(a)$ is strictly negative for every
finite $T>0$. As $T\to\infty$, its magnitude tends to zero because
$0<1-e^{-q_aT}<1$ and $q_a>0$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Occupation variance used in the simulations.}
Write $\pi=\share(a)$, $q=q_a$ and $d=1-e^{-qT}$. When $X_0=L$,
the joint probability of being in $H$ at times $s\leq t$ is
\[
 \Pr(X_s=H,X_t=H)
 =\pi(1-e^{-qs})\big[\pi+(1-\pi)e^{-q(t-s)}\big]
\]
by \eqref{eq:transition-matrix} and the Markov property. Squaring the
integral in $Y_T$ and applying Fubini's theorem gives, by symmetry off
the diagonal,
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E[Y_T^2]
 &=\frac{2}{T^2}\int_0^T\int_s^T
      \Pr(X_s=H,X_t=H)\,dt\,ds \notag\\
 &=\frac{2\pi}{T^2}\left[\pi I_1+\frac{1-\pi}{q}I_2\right],\label{eq:occupation-second-moment}\\
 I_1&=\int_0^T (1-e^{-qs})(T-s)\,ds
       =\frac{T^2}{2}-\frac{T}{q}+\frac{d}{q^2},\notag\\
 I_2&=\int_0^T(1-e^{-qs})(1-e^{-q(T-s)})\,ds
       =T(1+e^{-qT})-\frac{2d}{q}.\notag
\end{align}
In $I_1$, integration by parts gives
$\int_0^T e^{-qs}(T-s)\,ds=T/q-d/q^2$.
In $I_2$, the two single exponential terms each integrate to $d/q$ and
their product is the constant $e^{-qT}$. The variance is
$\mathbb E[Y_T^2]-\mathbb E[Y_T]^2$. For the mean of $N$ independent
replications, the variance is this quantity divided by $N$.

\subsection{Shape, return to the benchmark, and curvature}\label{app:shape}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}]
For $0\leq a\leq b$ the positive part in \eqref{eq:f} equals $b-a$, and
hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
 f(a)=b+(\eta b-1)a-\eta a^2,\qquad
 f'(a)=\eta(b-2a)-1,\qquad f''(a)=-2\eta .
\end{equation}
The derivatives are those of this branch, taken from within its domain
at the endpoints. With \eqref{eq:stationary}, the chain rule gives
\[
 \share'(a)=g'(\lam(a))\kappa f'(a)
           =\frac{\mu\kappa f'(a)}{[\lam(a)+\mu]^2}
\]
for $0\leq a<b$, as in \eqref{eq:derivative}. The denominator and the
factor $\mu\kappa$ are positive.

When $\eta=0$, $f'(a)=-1$ on the active branch. When $0<\eta b\leq1$,
$f'(a)=\eta b-1-2\eta a<0$ for $0<a<b$, with
$f'(0+)=0$ exactly when $\eta b=1$. The mean value theorem gives strict
decrease on $[0,b]$ in both regimes. When $\eta b>1$, the unique zero
of the affine derivative is $\peak$ in \eqref{eq:thresholds}. Since
$0<1/\eta<b$, the inequalities $0<\peak<b/2$ and
$0<\peak<2\peak=\ret<b$ follow. The derivative is positive before
$\peak$ and negative after it, giving the asserted strict monotonicity
on both subintervals by the mean value theorem.

For the no-AI comparison, $f(0)=b$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline-factor}
 f(a)-b=a[\eta(b-a)-1],\qquad 0\leq a\leq b .
\end{equation}
For $a>0$ on this branch, the bracket is negative under null or weak
complementarity. In the strong regime the bracket is $\eta(\ret-a)$,
and the signs in \eqref{eq:baseline} follow on this branch. At $a=0$
the shares agree by definition. For $a\geq b$, the positive part
gives $f(a)=0<b=f(0)$, proving \eqref{eq:plateau} and extending the
strict comparison to the entire plateau. Since the plateau lies
strictly below the no-AI share, the active-branch maxima already
identified are the unique global maxima.

The two branches of $f$ coincide at $b$, and the denominator in the
stationary share remains positive, giving continuity. The limit of the
derivative on the first branch is the left derivative in \eqref{eq:kink},
since $f(b)=0$ and $f'(b-)=-1-\eta b$. The plateau has right derivative
zero. On $0<a<b$, applying the chain rule again gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second}
 \share''(a)
 =-\frac{2\mu\kappa\eta}{(\lam(a)+\mu)^2}
  -\frac{2\mu\kappa^2[f'(a)]^2}{(\lam(a)+\mu)^3}<0 .
\end{equation}
For $\eta>0$ the first term is strictly negative, while for $\eta=0$
the second term is strictly negative because $f'=-1$. The strictly
decreasing derivative orders adjacent secant slopes strictly by the
mean value theorem, including slopes with an endpoint at $0$ or $b$.
This proves strict concavity on $[0,b]$. For any $0<\varepsilon<b$,
the plateau gives $\share(b+\varepsilon)=\share(b)$ and
$f(b-\varepsilon)>0$ gives $\share(b-\varepsilon)>\share(b)$. Thus
$\share(b)<[\share(b-\varepsilon)+\share(b+\varepsilon)]/2$, violating
the concavity inequality across $b$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Pairwise rankings and stochastic dominance}\label{app:pairwise}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise}]
Both $D_u$ and $D_v$ are positive. On putting the difference of
stationary shares over a common denominator, its numerator is
\begin{align*}
 \lam(v)[\lam(u)+\mu]-\lam(u)[\lam(v)+\mu]
 &=\mu[\lam(v)-\lam(u)]\\
 &=\mu\kappa[f(v)-f(u)].
\end{align*}
This proves \eqref{eq:globaldiff}. For $v\leq b$ (including $v=b$),
\eqref{eq:quadratic} applies at both points and gives
\begin{align*}
 f(v)-f(u)
 &= (\eta b-1)(v-u)-\eta(v^2-u^2)\\
 &= (v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1].
\end{align*}
Substitution gives \eqref{eq:pairwise}. The factors $\mu\kappa$ and
$v-u$ are strictly positive, giving the stated sign and equality
equivalences. If $u<b\leq v$, then $f(u)>0=f(v)$. At $v=b$ this
agrees with the active formula since its bracket is $-\eta u-1<0$.
If $b\leq u<v$, both effective efforts are zero, giving equality.

Write $F_w(y)=\Pr(s_{X}\leq y)$ for the cumulative distribution of
stationary skill at assistance $w$, with skill $s_X$ equal to $s_L$ in
$L$ and to $s_H$ in $H$. This distribution function is zero below $s_L$,
$1-\share(w)$ on $[s_L,s_H)$, and one at or above $s_H$. Thus
$F_v(y)\leq F_u(y)$ for every $y$ exactly when $\share(v)\geq\share(u)$,
with strict inequality at some $y$ exactly when $\share(v)>\share(u)$.
The two stationary laws agree exactly when their shares agree.
The difference in means is
$(s_H-s_L)[\share(v)-\share(u)]$, which has the sign of the share
difference because $s_H>s_L$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Thresholds, gains, and rate scaling}\label{app:sensitivity}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:sensitivity}]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}, the critical condition is $\eta b=1$,
so $\eta_c=1/b$. In \eqref{eq:thresholds}, differentiation of
$-1/\eta$ gives $1/\eta^2$, and differentiation of $b$ gives one.
This yields \eqref{eq:threshold-sensitivity}. Neither threshold
contains $\lambda_0,\kappa$, or $\mu$.

For a parameter $\theta$ that affects $\lam(a)$ while leaving $\mu$
fixed, the chain rule gives
\[
 \partial_\theta\share
 =\frac{\mu}{D^2}\partial_\theta\lam(a),\qquad
 D=\lam(a)+\mu .
\]
The derivatives of $\lam(a)$ in $\lambda_0,\kappa,\eta$ are, respectively,
$1,f(a),\kappa a(b-a)^+$. Since $1+\eta a>0$ and $a\geq0$, these
expressions give the stated signs and equality cases. At $\eta=0$
the derivative is taken from the right. For $b>a$,
$\partial_b\lam=\kappa(1+\eta a)$. For $b<a$, the positive part vanishes
locally, giving zero. At $b=a>0$ use the second branch for the left
derivative and the first branch for the right derivative. For fixed
$\lam(a)$, direct differentiation of $\lam(a)/D$ gives
$-\lam(a)/D^2$ in $\mu$. Finally, multiplying the rate parameters
by $c$ multiplies both $\lam(a)$ and $\mu$ by $c$. This factor cancels
in \eqref{eq:stationary}, while their sum becomes $cq_a$ in
\eqref{eq:transient}.

Setting $(u,v)=(0,a)$ in \eqref{eq:globaldiff} gives \eqref{eq:gain},
with $\Delta>0$ by \eqref{eq:baseline-factor}. At the maximum,
substituting $\peak=(\eta b-1)/(2\eta)$ into \eqref{eq:quadratic} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:peak-gain}
 f(\peak)-b=\frac{(\eta b-1)^2}{4\eta},\qquad
 \lam(\peak)-\lam(0)=\frac{\kappa(\eta b-1)^2}{4\eta}.
\end{equation}
Write $D_0=\lambda_0+\kappa b+\mu$, which is fixed when only $\eta$
varies. Then \eqref{eq:gain} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:peak-gain-limit}
 \lim_{\eta\downarrow 1/b}
  \frac{G(\peak)}{(\eta b-1)^2}
 =\lim_{\eta\downarrow 1/b}
  \frac{\mu\kappa}{4\eta D_0\big[D_0+\kappa(\eta b-1)^2/(4\eta)\big]}
 =\frac{\mu\kappa b}{4D_0^2}>0 .
\end{equation}
For the rate derivatives of $G(a)$, keep $a,b,\eta$ fixed. Define
$C_1=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)$, $C_0=\lambda_0+\kappa b$,
$M=(C_1+\mu)(C_0+\mu)$ and $B=\lambda_0+\mu$, all strictly positive.
The product and quotient rules give
\begin{align}
 \partial_{\lambda_0}G
 &=-\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta[(C_1+\mu)+(C_0+\mu)]}{M^2}<0,\notag\\
 \partial_\mu G
 &=\frac{\kappa\Delta\,[M-\mu(C_1+C_0+2\mu)]}{M^2}
  =\frac{\kappa\Delta(C_1C_0-\mu^2)}{M^2},\label{eq:gain-derivatives}\\
 \partial_\kappa G
 &=\frac{\mu\Delta\,[M-\kappa(B(f(a)+b)+2\kappa f(a)b)]}{M^2}
  =\frac{\mu\Delta[B^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b]}{M^2}.\notag
\end{align}
For the last equality use $M=(B+\kappa f(a))(B+\kappa b)$.
The factors outside the sign brackets are positive, including
$\Delta$. As $\mu$ increases, its sign bracket decreases through
zero at $\sqrt{C_1C_0}$. As $\kappa$ increases, its bracket decreases
through zero at $B/\sqrt{f(a)b}$. In each case the derivative is positive
below the root and negative above it, giving a unique maximum.
For the dimensionless form, set $z=a/b$ and $r=\eta b$.
Then $f(a)=b(1-z)^+(1+rz)$. Dividing the numerator and denominator
of \eqref{eq:stationary} by $\mu$ shows that the share depends on rates
through $\lambda_0/\mu$ and $\kappa b/\mu$.
\end{proof}

$\pi_H(\HandHighAssistance)=\HandHighShare<\HandBaselineShare=\pi_H(0)$.
At the peak, $\pi_H(\HandPeak)=\HandPeakShare$.
The conjecture is therefore false even with positive effort. The same
witness is checked with exact rational arithmetic in \path{code/verify.py}.

The author also supplied six numbered photographs, archived in order in
\href{https://github.com/wbgradost/ai-project/tree/main/hand}{\texttt{hand/}}.
They cover the five derivations in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The review
found a sign slip in part~2. The photograph writes $h_0+\pi_H$
where \eqref{eq:transient} and the preceding handwritten step give $h_0-\pi_H$.
Part~5 gives the maximizing value of $\kappa$ but omits the intermediate
derivation of $\partial_\kappa G$ in \eqref{eq:gain-derivatives}.
The repository preserves the original photographs and the separate
counterexample in Figure~\ref{fig:hand}.
