\begin{abstract}
\noindent Can AI assistance improve long-run skill while displacing human effort?
I preserve the myopic worker problem of Aouad, Lykouris, and Zhong (2026) and
replace only the learning rate with $\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a)$.
In a two-state continuous-time Markov chain, let $b>0$ be low-state effort
without AI. The stationary high-skill share initially increases if and only if
$\eta b>1$. Its unique maximum is then at $(b-1/\eta)/2$, its return to
the no-AI share is at $b-1/\eta$, and effort vanishes at $b$. Exact pairwise
comparisons distinguish marginal reversal from gains relative to no AI.
Reproducible formula evaluations illustrate the mechanism; independent
Markov trajectories check predicted occupation shares with explicit Monte
Carlo error and initial-condition bias. Mathematical proofs are complete
here. The official Lean workflow has not run; no result is reported as
Lean verified.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
AI can substitute for human effort in current production while making the
remaining effort more effective for learning. These effects need not have
the same implications for the long-run distribution of skill. This paper
asks when the second effect is strong enough to reverse the first. The answer
is conditional and local: in the two-state model studied here, the stationary
high-skill share rises over an initial interval precisely when the product
of learning complementarity and low-state effort without AI exceeds one.

The starting point is Proposition~3.5 of \citet[p.~8]{aouad2026paradoxes}.
In their endogenous-skill model, assistance reduces effort; an upward learning
rate that depends increasingly on effort alone then falls. Lower assistance
therefore first-order stochastically dominates higher assistance in the
stationary skill distribution. I retain their worker objective, myopia,
additive production inputs, and constant downward transition rate. The only
modified primitive is the learning technology: AI enters it directly and
complements effort. For each fixed assistance level it remains positive,
increasing, and affine in effort.

The contribution is a complete two-state characterization, including the
corner at zero effort. Three comparisons have different thresholds: the
marginal effect of more assistance, the comparison with no assistance, and
the comparison of two positive assistance levels. Confusing them would
overstate what complementarity achieves. The stationary high-skill share can
remain above its no-AI value after its marginal assistance effect has become
negative. Sufficiently high assistance eliminates the effort channel entirely.

This is a working manuscript for critique, not a claim that the November
final-paper deliverable is complete. Mathematical statements and proofs
identify a future formalization target. Numerical checks and stochastic
simulations respond to feedback on the topic presentation. The author's
handwritten derivations and the official paper-scoped Lean run remain
pending; their status is reported explicitly.

\section{Relation to the baseline and established literature}
The baseline is the reviewed arXiv version~1 of
\citet{aouad2026paradoxes}, especially Proposition~2.1 (effort),
Proposition~3.5 (stationary skill degradation), and Appendix~A.4 (its proof).
That comparison orders stationary distributions through upward transition
rates that fall with assistance. In this extension an upward rate can instead
increase, even though optimal effort continues to fall. With two ordered
states, the distributional comparison reduces to the high-state probability.
This restriction permits an exact result; no $N$-state extension is asserted.

The topic-stage review distinguished this contribution from
\citet{davies2026course}, a working paper in the reviewed September 2026
version~3. Davies studies course design, work versus delegation, and
state-dependent effects of AI quality on second-best per-task effort and
skill acquisition under his stated conditions. This paper does not claim to
introduce AI--learning complementarity. It gives closed-form stationary
thresholds in the different birth--death framework of Aouad et al.
The experimental studies reviewed at the topic stage
\citep{shen2026skill,wu2026assistance} motivate distinguishing active use from
delegation; they do not estimate this paper's parameters or validate its
stationary comparative static. Bibliographic version descriptions refer to
the sources reviewed at that stage, rather than a new claim about current
editorial status.

\section{Model and assumptions}\label{sec:model}
Assistance $a$ is fixed and exogenous, held constant as the skill process
evolves. The worker observes current skill $s$ and chooses effort $e\geq0$
to maximize current utility. There is no continuation value in this choice.
Skill changes subsequently through the rates specified below.

\begin{assumption}[Worker problem and selection]\label{ass:worker}
The production function $p:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is continuously
differentiable, nondecreasing, and concave. Effort costs $\gamma e$, where
$\gamma>0$. The growth condition is
\[
 \limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{p(x)}{x}<\gamma.
\]
The worker selects the largest effort among maximizers of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:worker}
 \max_{e\geq0}\{p(s+e+a)-\gamma e\},\qquad s,a\geq0.
\end{equation}
Write $x^*=\max\arg\max_{x\geq0}\{p(x)-\gamma x\}$.
\end{assumption}
The selection rule preserves the baseline convention under weak concavity;
uniqueness is not silently imposed. The growth restriction guarantees a
nonempty compact maximizer set. At an interior optimum the FOC is
$p'(s+e+a)=\gamma$. Learning cannot change this condition because it is
absent from the myopic objective.

\begin{assumption}[States, learning, and depreciation]\label{ass:chain}
There are two skills $0\leq s_L<s_H$, with $b:=x^*-s_L>0$.
For fixed $a\geq0$, the skill process is a time-homogeneous continuous-time
Markov chain. Its transition rate from $L$ to $H$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:technology}
 \lambda(e,a)=\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a),\qquad
 \lambda_0>0,\quad\kappa>0,\quad\eta\geq0,
\end{equation}
evaluated at optimal low-state effort. The rate from $H$ to $L$ is the
constant $\mu>0$. There is no state above $H$.
\end{assumption}
High-state effort can affect current production, but cannot generate another
upward transition in this chain. Baseline learning $\lambda_0$ does not
require measured effort. The parameter $\kappa$ converts effective effort
into a transition intensity; $\eta$ is the increase in the learning
effectiveness of effort per unit of assistance. At fixed $a$,
$\lambda_e=\kappa(1+\eta a)>0$ and $\lambda_{ee}=0$;
$\lambda_{ea}=\kappa\eta\geq0$. Joint concavity in $(e,a)$ is neither
assumed nor used.

\begin{definition}[Effective effort]\label{def:f}
For $a\geq0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
 f(a)=(b-a)^+(1+\eta a),\qquad \lam(a)=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a).
\end{equation}
Let $\share(a)$ be the stationary probability of $H$, and let
$\pi_L(a)=1-\share(a)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Effort under the baseline selection rule]\label{lem:effort}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:worker}, selected effort is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effort}
 e^*(s,a)=(x^*-s-a)^+.
\end{equation}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}, $e_L^*(a)=b-a>0$ for $0\leq a<b$;
$e_L^*(a)=0$ for $a\geq b$. On $(0,b)$ its derivative is $-1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $z=s+a$ and $q(x)=p(x)-\gamma x$. Replacing $e$ with $x=z+e$
rewrites the objective as $q(x)+\gamma z$ on $x\geq z$.
The growth restriction gives $q(x)\to-\infty$. Continuity gives a
nonempty compact global maximizer set, with largest member $x^*$.
If $z\leq x^*$, this member is feasible and remains the largest
constrained maximizer. If $z>x^*$, $q$ is strictly decreasing on
$[z,\infty)$. Indeed, take $x^*<x<y$. If $q(y)\geq q(x)$, concavity
would imply
\[
 \frac{q(x)-q(x^*)}{x-x^*}\geq
 \frac{q(y)-q(x)}{y-x}\geq0,
\]
contradicting $q(x)<q(x^*)$, since $x$ is beyond the largest global
maximizer. The constrained maximizer is then $z$. Thus the selected total
input is $\max\{x^*,z\}$, yielding \eqref{eq:effort}. Substitute $s=s_L$
and use $b>0$ for the last claims. Since $x^*>s_L\geq0$,
differentiability at the interior maximizer also gives $p'(x^*)=\gamma$.
No strict concavity is needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Stationarity and finite-horizon occupation]\label{lem:stationary}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, each fixed $a\geq0$
has a unique stationary distribution,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
 \share(a)=\frac{\lam(a)}{\lam(a)+\mu},\qquad
 \pi_L(a)=\frac{\mu}{\lam(a)+\mu}.
\end{equation}
Write $q_a=\lam(a)+\mu>0$. If $h_0\in[0,1]$ is the initial probability
of $H$, its probability at time $t\geq0$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transient}
 h(t)=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))e^{-q_a t}.
\end{equation}
For $T>0$, the occupation fraction
$Y_T=T^{-1}\int_0^T\mathbf1_{\{X_t=H\}}\,dt$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occupation}
 \mathbb E[Y_T]=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))
                       \frac{1-e^{-q_aT}}{q_aT}.
\end{equation}
Starting in $L$ therefore creates a downward bias of
$\share(a)(1-e^{-q_aT})/(q_aT)$ relative to stationarity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The generator in order $(L,H)$ is
\[
 Q(a)=\begin{pmatrix}-\lam(a)&\lam(a)\\ \mu&-\mu\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both off-diagonal rates are strictly positive by
Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}; the finite chain is irreducible. Stationary
balance and normalization are
$\pi_L\lam=\share\mu$ and $\pi_L+\share=1$.
They imply $(\lam+\mu)\share=\lam$. Division is valid because
$\lam\geq\lambda_0>0$ and $\mu>0$. The resulting probabilities are
positive, sum to one, and uniquely solve the two equations.
The forward equation is
$h'=\lam(1-h)-\mu h=\lam-q_a h$.
Subtract $\share=\lam/q_a$ to obtain
$(h-\share)'=-q_a(h-\share)$, with unique solution
\eqref{eq:transient}. Boundedness of the indicator allows expectation and
integration to be interchanged. Integrating \eqref{eq:transient} on $[0,T]$
and dividing by $T$ proves \eqref{eq:occupation}. Set $h_0=0$ for the
stated bias. These formulas show convergence of the marginal probability and
the expected occupation to stationarity.
\end{proof}

\section{Results: three distinct comparisons}\label{sec:results}
For $\mu>0$ the map $g(\lambda)=\lambda/(\lambda+\mu)$ is strictly
increasing, with $g'(\lambda)=\mu/(\lambda+\mu)^2>0$.
Every stationary comparison therefore has the sign of its learning-rate
comparison. Positivity of $\mu$ matters: with $\mu=0$ irreducibility is
lost and the stationary share no longer encodes these strict comparisons.

\begin{proposition}[Global shape, maximum, and comparison with no AI]\label{prop:shape}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, $\share$ is continuous
on $[0,\infty)$. For $0\leq a<b$, its derivative (the right derivative
at $0$) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative}
 \share'(a)=\frac{\mu\kappa[\eta(b-2a)-1]}{[\lam(a)+\mu]^2}.
\end{equation}
The following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item If $\eta b\leq1$, $\share$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,b]$,
 has its unique global maximum at $a=0$, and
 $\share(a)<\share(0)$ for all $a>0$.
 If $\eta b=1$, $\share'(0+)=0$ but $\share'(a)<0$ on $(0,b)$.
 \item If $\eta b>1$, define
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:thresholds}
 \peak=\frac{b-1/\eta}{2},\qquad \ret=b-1/\eta=2\peak.
 \end{equation}
 Then $0<\peak<\ret<b$ and $\peak<b/2$; $\share$ is strictly
 increasing on $[0,\peak]$, strictly decreasing on $[\peak,b]$, and
 has its unique global maximum at $\peak$. Moreover,
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline}
 \share(a)>\share(0)\iff0<a<\ret,\qquad
 \share(\ret)=\share(0),\qquad
 \share(a)<\share(0)\quad(a>\ret).
 \end{equation}
 \item In either regime, for all $a\geq b$, effort is zero and
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:plateau}
 \lam(a)=\lambda_0,\qquad
 \share(a)=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_0+\mu}<\share(0).
 \end{equation}
 At $b$ the one-sided derivatives are
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:kink}
 \share'(b-)=-\frac{\mu\kappa(1+\eta b)}{(\lambda_0+\mu)^2}<0,
 \qquad \share'(b+)=0.
 \end{equation}
 On $(0,b)$, $\share''<0$; this does not imply global concavity across $b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On $[0,b)$ expand
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
 f(a)=b+(\eta b-1)a-\eta a^2,\qquad
 f'(a)=\eta(b-2a)-1,\qquad f''(a)=-2\eta.
\end{equation}
The quotient and chain rules give \eqref{eq:derivative}; its denominator is
positive and $\mu\kappa>0$. When $\eta b\leq1$, $f'(a)<0$ for
every $a\in(0,b)$: if $\eta=0$, it equals $-1$, and otherwise
$\eta b-1-2\eta a<0$. The mean value theorem gives strict decrease,
including endpoint $0$ even when its right derivative vanishes. At $b$,
$f(a)>0=f(b)$ for each $a<b$ extends the strict comparison.

When $\eta b>1$, necessarily $\eta>0$. Solving $f'(a)=0$ gives
$\peak$. The condition $b>1/\eta$ gives $\peak>0$; $1/\eta>0$
gives $\peak<b/2$, and $0<\peak<2\peak=\ret<b$.
The derivative is positive before $\peak$ and negative after it. Strict
monotonicity of $g$ transfers these comparisons to $\share$.
For $0<a<b$, subtract $f(0)=b$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline-factor}
 f(a)-b=a[\eta(b-a)-1].
\end{equation}
If $\eta b\leq1$, the bracket is negative for $a>0$. If
$\eta b>1$, it is positive exactly for $a<\ret$, zero at $\ret$,
and negative thereafter. This proves the active-region no-AI comparisons.
At $a=0$ the shares agree, so a strict gain excludes zero.

For $a\geq b$, the positive part in \eqref{eq:f} vanishes, proving
\eqref{eq:plateau}. Since $f(0)=b>0$, the plateau lies strictly below
the no-AI share. The positive part is continuous, and the rational
denominator remains positive, proving continuity at $b$.
Take $a\uparrow b$ in \eqref{eq:derivative} to obtain the left derivative
in \eqref{eq:kink}. The right derivative is zero because $\share$ is
constant on $[b,\infty)$. These signs and the plateau also establish
uniqueness of the claimed global maxima. On $(0,b)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second}
 \share''(a)=-\frac{2\mu\kappa\eta}{(\lam+\mu)^2}
             -\frac{2\mu\kappa^2[f'(a)]^2}{(\lam+\mu)^3}<0.
\end{equation}
If $\eta>0$, the first term is strictly negative; if $\eta=0$,
$f'=-1$ makes the second strictly negative. The upward derivative jump at
$b$ prevents a global concavity conclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Comparison of two assistance levels]\label{prop:pairwise}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, for any $0\leq u<v$,
with $D_w=\lambda_0+\kappa f(w)+\mu>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:globaldiff}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=\frac{\mu\kappa[f(v)-f(u)]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
If $v\leq b$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairwise}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=
 \frac{\mu\kappa(v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\share(v)>\share(u)$ if and only if $\eta(b-u-v)>1$;
the analogous equality and strict-reverse equivalences also hold.
If $u<b\leq v$, $\share(v)<\share(u)$.
If $b\leq u<v$, the shares agree.
In two states, the share comparison also characterizes first-order stochastic
dominance and the ordering of mean skill.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the fractions in \eqref{eq:stationary}. The common numerator is
$\lam(v)[\lam(u)+\mu]-\lam(u)[\lam(v)+\mu]
=\mu[\lam(v)-\lam(u)]$, proving \eqref{eq:globaldiff}.
For $v\leq b$, the quadratic in \eqref{eq:quadratic} is valid at both
points, including $v=b$, so
\[
 f(v)-f(u)=(\eta b-1)(v-u)-\eta(v^2-u^2)
          =(v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1].
\]
The factors $D_uD_v$, $\mu\kappa$, and $v-u$ are positive, yielding
all sign equivalences. If $u<b\leq v$, then $f(u)>0=f(v)$; if
$b\leq u<v$, both equal zero. Finally, the cumulative probability
between $s_L$ and $s_H$ is $1-\share$, and expected skill is
$s_L+(s_H-s_L)\share$. Since $s_H>s_L$, these comparisons are exactly
the share comparison. This is a two-state statement.
\end{proof}
An increase from $u$ to $v$ can improve skill even when $v$ exceeds the
maximum: in the strong regime the exact condition is $u+v<2\peak$.
A marginal comparison at $v$ instead requires $v<\peak$. The no-AI
comparison sets $u=0$ and requires $v<2\peak$. These statements answer
different questions; none says that every positive increase improves skill.

\begin{corollary}[Threshold locations and outcome magnitudes]\label{cor:sensitivity}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, critical complementarity
is $\eta_c=1/b$. In the strong regime the thresholds $\peak$ and $\ret$
depend only on $\eta,b$, independently of $\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu$.
Holding the other arguments fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-sensitivity}
 \partial_\eta\peak=\frac{1}{2\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_\eta\ret=\frac{1}{\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_b\peak=\frac12,\quad \partial_b\ret=1.
\end{equation}
For fixed $a\geq0$ and $D=\lam(a)+\mu$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:level-sensitivity}
 \partial_{\lambda_0}\share=\frac{\mu}{D^2}>0,\qquad
 \partial_\kappa\share=\frac{\mu f(a)}{D^2}\geq0,\qquad
 \partial_\mu\share=-\frac{\lam(a)}{D^2}<0.
\end{equation}
Also $\partial_\eta\share=\mu\kappa a(b-a)^+/D^2\geq0$
(a right derivative at $\eta=0$);
for $a<b$, $\partial_b\share=\mu\kappa(1+\eta a)/D^2>0$.
Multiplying all three rates $(\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu)$ by the same
$c>0$ leaves the entire stationary curve unchanged and multiplies the
convergence rate $q_a$ by $c$.

For a fixed $0<a<\ret$ in the strong regime, the positive no-AI gain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
 G(a):=\share(a)-\share(0)
 =\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta}{(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)+\mu)
                             (\lambda_0+\kappa b+\mu)},\qquad
 \Delta=f(a)-b>0.
\end{equation}
It strictly decreases with $\lambda_0$. Its derivative in $\mu$ has the
sign of $(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a))(\lambda_0+\kappa b)-\mu^2$;
its derivative in $\kappa$ has the sign of
$(\lambda_0+\mu)^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b$.
Thus stronger effort sensitivity or faster depreciation need not
monotonically increase the gain, even though they leave its assistance
thresholds unchanged.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The regime boundary and thresholds follow from
Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}; differentiation gives
\eqref{eq:threshold-sensitivity}. Their independence of the other rates is
literal. Apply the quotient rule in \eqref{eq:stationary}, using
$\partial_\kappa\lam=f$, $\partial_{\lambda_0}\lam=1$,
$\partial_\eta\lam=\kappa a(b-a)^+$, and, for $a<b$,
$\partial_b\lam=\kappa(1+\eta a)$, to obtain the level formulas.
For common scaling, cancel $c$ in $c\lam/(c\lam+c\mu)$; the sum of the
rates becomes $cq_a$, proving the convergence statement using
Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}.
Setting $u=0$ in \eqref{eq:globaldiff} proves \eqref{eq:gain}.
Let $C_1=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)>0$, $C_0=\lambda_0+\kappa b>0$,
$M=(C_1+\mu)(C_0+\mu)>0$, and $B=\lambda_0+\mu>0$.
Holding $a,b,\eta$ fixed, differentiation gives
\[
 \partial_{\lambda_0}G
 =-\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta[(C_1+\mu)+(C_0+\mu)]}{M^2}<0,
 \qquad
 \partial_\mu G=\frac{\kappa\Delta(C_1C_0-\mu^2)}{M^2},
\]
\[
 \partial_\kappa G=\frac{\mu\Delta[B^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b]}{M^2}.
\]
All prefactors and denominators are positive. This establishes the stated
signs and equality cases without assuming a globally monotone gain.
\end{proof}
Threshold invariance holds conditional on fixed $b,\eta$, not on arbitrary
changes to the worker problem. For the numerical example
$p(x)=A\log(1+x)$, $A>\gamma>0$, one has
$x^*=A/\gamma-1$ and $b=A/\gamma-1-s_L>0$.
Raising $A$ increases $b$; raising $\gamma$ or $s_L$ lowers it.
The stationary curve depends on the dimensionless arguments
$a/b$, $\eta b$, $\lambda_0/\mu$, and $\kappa b/\mu$, as follows by
dividing \eqref{eq:stationary} by $\mu$ and factoring $b$ from $f$.

\section{Numerical evidence and stochastic validation}\label{sec:sim}
\subsection{Design and economic interpretation}
All parameters are illustrative, not estimated or calibrated to data.
Use $p(x)=4\log(1+x)$, $\gamma=1$, $s_L=1$, and $s_H=3$;
then $x^*=3$ and $b=2$. Set $\lambda_0=0.1$, $\kappa=0.4$, and
$\mu=0.3$, with rates per chosen time unit. Without measured effort the
mean upward waiting time is ten units; the mean high-state spell is
$1/0.3$ units. These units have no assigned calendar interpretation.
Assistance and effort have the same production-input units.

We compare $\eta\in\{0,0.25,1\}$, so
$\eta b\in\{0,0.5,2\}$. The first regime excludes complementarity;
the second allows it below the critical value; the third crosses the
condition in Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}. A separate equality check uses
$\eta=0.5$, testing the zero derivative at $a=0$ without misclassifying it
as a gain region.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../code/figures/mechanism.pdf}
 \caption{Formula evaluations of effort, learning, and stationary skill.
 Effort is identical across regimes. Only strong complementarity makes the
 learning rate and high-skill share rise initially. These curves are not
 stochastic simulations. Source: \texttt{code/verify.py}.}
 \label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} separates behavior from learning. Effort falls
one-for-one until $a=2$ in every regime: the learning technology does not
alter that response. Without complementarity learning falls immediately.
Weak complementarity attenuates the fall but cannot reverse it. Strong
complementarity raises learning for $0<a<0.5$ even as effort falls.
Beyond the maximum, additional effectiveness no longer offsets displaced
effort. Every regime meets the same zero-effort plateau.

In the strong example, the maximum is at $a=0.5$, the return to the no-AI
share at $a=1$, and effort disappears at $a=2$. The no-AI share is $3/4$;
the maximum is $10/13$, a gain of $1/52$, approximately $1.9231$
percentage points. The plateau is $1/4$. Indeed, $f(0)=2$,
$f(0.5)=2.25$, $\lam(0)=0.9$, and $\lam(0.5)=1.0$.
Between $0.5$ and $1$, assistance lowers skill at the margin while the
share remains above its no-AI value.

Figure~\ref{fig:thresholds} shows which parameters determine locations.
A larger $\eta b$ creates and expands the gain regions, but the marginal

as complementarity grows, but never reaches it for finite $\eta$.
The rates $\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu$ do not move these boundaries: they
multiply or monotonically transform the same effective-effort comparison.
Production parameters can move them through $b$.

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} separates location from magnitude. Baseline
learning raises all shares; depreciation lowers all shares. Neither changes
where $\share'(a)$ changes sign. A large stationary share need not imply a
large complementarity gain: high baseline learning compresses the gain over
no AI. The gain can also be small when increased effort sensitivity drives
both compared shares toward one. Corollary~\ref{cor:sensitivity} explains
why the gain need not be monotone in $\kappa$ or $\mu$.

\subsection{Continuous-time Markov simulations}
Each path draws an exponential holding time with its current state's exit
rate, then switches to the other state. There is no discrete-time transition
approximation. Occupation is time spent in $H$, including the final censored
holding spell, divided by the horizon. Counting the proportion of visits to
$H$ would be inappropriate: the embedded jump chain alternates and does not
have the same occupation law.

For each of $21$ assistance/regime cells, independent paths start in $L$.
There are \MCReplications{} replications per cell and horizons
$T=25,100,400$. The cells combine the three complementarity values with
$a\in\{0,0.25,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}$. The master seed is $20261104$;
independent child streams make the experiment reproducible. No burn-in is
hidden. Each path is evaluated at all three horizons, so estimates at
different horizons share a trajectory; errors at a given horizon are computed
across independent paths. Means are compared with the stationary share and with the exact
finite-horizon expectation from Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}. The standard
error is the sample standard deviation of independent path occupation
fractions divided by the square root of the replication count. Plotted
$95\%$ intervals use this replication-level error, rather than treating
autocorrelated within-path observations as independent.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The mechanism is a contest between two terms:
\[
 \lam'(a)=\underbrace{\kappa\eta(b-a)}_{\text{effectiveness of retained effort}}
          -\underbrace{\kappa(1+\eta a)}_{\text{displaced effort}},\qquad a<b.
\]
The FOC never changes. Complementarity affects the return generated by the
effort the myopic worker still chooses. The condition $\eta b>1$ thus
depends on both the technology and no-AI effort. It is not a welfare theorem,
an empirical estimate, or a global recommendation for assistance. Output,
effort costs, stationary skill, and social welfare need not share an ordering.
The maintained interiority condition $b>0$ is essential for a strict reversal:
if $b\leq0$, the same effort rule gives $e_L^*(a)=0$ for every $a\geq0$,
and the stationary share is constant. Positive $\kappa$ and $\mu$ are also
needed for strict comparative statics; the proof does not cover a model
without depreciation or without an effort-sensitive learning channel.

The two-state restriction is substantial. The value of $s_H$ does not enter
transition rates or the high-state share, although it affects mean skill and
production. With more states, different effort cutoffs could make upward
rates move in opposite directions; a single high-state probability would
not characterize the stationary ordering. A third-state illustration is
omitted to keep the verified contribution complete and interpretable.

The proof resolves stationarity, derivative signs, equality cases, the effort
boundary, and all pairwise comparisons. Delicate steps were preserving the
weak-concavity selection rule and distinguishing the maximum from the no-AI
return. Extending the active quadratic beyond $b$ would produce negative
effort and a false global result. Global concavity is also false because the
derivative jumps upward to zero at $b$. Operationally pending are independent
author-written verification, the official Lean run against a fixed PDF, and
incorporation of class critique. No unexecuted Lean fragment is offered as a
certificate.

\section{Conclusion}
AI--effort complementarity reverses stationary skill degradation over an
initial assistance interval exactly when $\eta b>1$. The marginal maximum,
return to the no-AI benchmark, and zero-effort boundary are different objects.
Analytical proofs establish these comparisons; formula evaluations explain
their magnitudes, while continuous-time simulations test occupation behavior
with explicit Monte Carlo error and finite-horizon bias. This manuscript
defines substantive targets for the course's subsequent paper-scoped Lean
procedure.
