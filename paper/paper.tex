\begin{abstract}
\noindent Can AI assistance improve long-run skill while displacing human effort?
I preserve the myopic worker problem of Aouad, Lykouris, and Zhong (2026) and
replace only the learning rate with $\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a)$.
In a two-state continuous-time Markov chain, let $b>0$ be low-state effort
without AI. The stationary high-skill share initially increases if and only if
$\eta b>1$. Its unique maximum is then at $(b-1/\eta)/2$, its return to
the no-AI share is at $b-1/\eta$, and effort vanishes at $b$. Exact pairwise
comparisons distinguish marginal reversal from gains relative to no AI.
Numerical evaluations illustrate the thresholds and gain magnitudes.
Simulations of the skill process compare realized occupation shares with the
finite-horizon prediction and separate Monte Carlo error from the effect of
initial skill.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
AI can substitute for human effort in current production while making the
remaining effort more effective for learning. These effects need not have
the same implications for the long-run distribution of skill. This paper
asks when the second effect is strong enough to reverse the first. In the
two-state model, the stationary high-skill share rises over an initial
interval precisely when the product of learning complementarity and
low-state effort without AI exceeds one. At sufficiently high assistance,
the share falls below its no-AI value.

The starting point is Proposition~3.5 of \citet[p.~8]{aouad2026paradoxes}.
In their endogenous-skill model, assistance reduces effort; an upward learning
rate that depends increasingly on effort alone then falls. Lower assistance
therefore first-order stochastically dominates higher assistance in the
stationary skill distribution in the weak sense. I retain their worker
objective, myopia, additive production inputs, and constant downward
transition rate. Within the two-state specialization, the only modified
primitive is the learning technology, where AI raises the learning
productivity of effort. For each fixed assistance level the rate remains positive,
increasing, and affine in effort.

The contribution is a complete two-state characterization, including the
corner at zero effort. Three comparisons have different thresholds: the
marginal effect of more assistance, the comparison with no assistance, and
the comparison of two positive assistance levels. Confusing them would
overstate what complementarity achieves. The stationary high-skill share can
remain above its no-AI value after its marginal assistance effect has become
negative. Sufficiently high assistance eliminates the effort channel entirely.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
The reference result is Proposition~3.5 (p.~8) of the May 2026 arXiv
version~1 of \citet{aouad2026paradoxes}, proved in its Appendix~A.4.
The worker takes deterministic additive assistance and current skill as
given, maximizes current utility with concave production and a linear
effort cost, and selects the largest maximizer. Their Proposition~2.1
establishes the effort rule and their Corollary~2.2 gives weak effort
displacement. In the finite ordered skill model,
upward rates are positive, increasing and weakly concave functions of
optimal effort alone, and the downward rate is a common positive constant.
For two fixed assistance levels, the higher level weakly reduces each
adjacent upward-to-downward rate ratio. The resulting stationary skill
distribution is weakly first-order stochastically dominated by the
distribution at lower assistance. Equal rates can give equal distributions.
The \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11350v2#S3.SS2}{October 2026 arXiv revision}
renumbers the result as Proposition~3.3
and allows skill to interact with AI in production. The additive model
and proposition numbering used here refer to version~1. A conference
version was accepted at EC 2026.

\citet{davies2026course} studies work versus delegation in course design.
When AI complements effort, higher AI quality can speed up or slow down
learning depending on the student's skill and the course designer's
incentive constraint. The present paper characterizes the thresholds
for a fixed assistance level in a stationary birth--death model.
In a programming experiment, \citet{shen2026skill} find that uses of AI
involving active cognitive engagement can preserve performance on a
subsequent unaided assessment. In a logic-puzzle experiment,
\citet{wu2026assistance} find an association between independent
problem-solving during AI access and greater subsequent skill gains.
These studies motivate the distinction between effort displacement and
learning effectiveness. The model's transition rates and long-run
comparisons remain theoretical objects.

\section{Model and assumptions}\label{sec:model}
Assistance $a$ is fixed and exogenous, held constant as the skill process
evolves. The worker observes current skill $s$ and chooses effort $e\geq0$
to maximize current utility. There is no continuation value in this choice.
Skill changes subsequently through the rates specified below.

\begin{assumption}[Worker problem and selection]\label{ass:worker}
The production function $p:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is continuously
differentiable, nondecreasing, and concave. Effort costs $\gamma e$, where
$\gamma>0$. The growth condition is
\[
 \limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{p(x)}{x}<\gamma.
\]
The worker selects the largest effort among maximizers of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:worker}
 \max_{e\geq0}\{p(s+e+a)-\gamma e\},\qquad s,a\geq0.
\end{equation}
Write $x^*=\max\arg\max_{x\geq0}\{p(x)-\gamma x\}$.
\end{assumption}
The selection rule preserves the baseline convention under weak concavity.
Continuous first derivatives suffice for the effort argument here.
Optimal effort can be set-valued before selection. The growth restriction
ensures existence, and Lemma~\ref{lem:effort} establishes the selected
effort for every feasible state and assistance level.

\begin{assumption}[States, learning, and depreciation]\label{ass:chain}
There are two skills $0\leq s_L<s_H$, with $b:=x^*-s_L>0$.
For fixed $a\geq0$, the skill process is a time-homogeneous continuous-time
Markov chain. Its transition rate from $L$ to $H$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:technology}
 \lambda(e,a)=\lambda_0+\kappa e(1+\eta a),\qquad
 \lambda_0>0,\quad\kappa>0,\quad\eta\geq0,
\end{equation}
evaluated at optimal low-state effort. The rate from $H$ to $L$ is the
constant $\mu>0$. There is no state above $H$.
\end{assumption}
High-state effort can affect current production, but cannot generate another
upward transition in this chain. Baseline learning $\lambda_0$ does not
require measured effort. The parameter $\kappa$ converts effective effort
into a transition intensity; $\eta$ is the increase in the learning
effectiveness of effort per unit of assistance. At fixed $a$,
$\lambda_e=\kappa(1+\eta a)>0$ and $\lambda_{ee}=0$;
$\lambda_{ea}=\kappa\eta\geq0$. The technology is defined for $e,a\geq0$.
Skill, effort and assistance have the same production-input units. The
rates $\lambda_0$ and $\mu$ are in inverse time units, $\kappa$ is a
rate per unit of effective effort, and $\eta$ has inverse assistance
units. We call complementarity absent if $\eta=0$, weak if $0<\eta b\leq1$,
and strong if $\eta b>1$. The boundary $\eta b=1$ is the critical weak case.

\begin{definition}[Effective effort]\label{def:f}
For any real $y$, write $y^+=\max\{y,0\}$. For $a\geq0$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
 f(a)=(b-a)^+(1+\eta a),\qquad \lam(a)=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a).
\end{equation}
Let $\share(a)$ be the stationary probability of $H$, and let
$\pi_L(a)=1-\share(a)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Effort under the baseline selection rule]\label{lem:effort}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:worker}, selected effort is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effort}
 e^*(s,a)=(x^*-s-a)^+.
\end{equation}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}, $e_L^*(a)=b-a>0$ for $0\leq a<b$;
$e_L^*(a)=0$ for $a\geq b$. On $(0,b)$ its derivative is $-1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $z=s+a$ and $q(x)=p(x)-\gamma x$. Replacing $e$ with $x=z+e$
rewrites the objective as $q(x)+\gamma z$ on $x\geq z$.
By the growth restriction there are $\delta>0$ and $R<\infty$ such
that $q(x)\leq-\delta x$ for $x\geq R$. Increasing $R$ if necessary
makes $q(x)<q(0)$ for $x\geq R$. Continuity and maximization on $[0,R]$
then give a nonempty compact global maximizer set, with largest member
$x^*$. Concavity makes the set an interval, possibly a singleton.
If $z\leq x^*$, this member is feasible and remains the largest
constrained maximizer. If $z>x^*$, $q$ is strictly decreasing on
$[z,\infty)$. Indeed, take $x^*<x<y$. If $q(y)\geq q(x)$, concavity
would imply
\[
 \frac{q(x)-q(x^*)}{x-x^*}\geq
 \frac{q(y)-q(x)}{y-x}\geq0,
\]
contradicting $q(x)<q(x^*)$, since $x$ is beyond the largest global
maximizer. The constrained maximizer is then uniquely $z$. At $z=x^*$,
any strictly larger total input gives a strictly smaller objective, so
zero is also the unique constrained effort at the cutoff. Thus the selected total
input is $\max\{x^*,z\}$, yielding \eqref{eq:effort}. Substitute $s=s_L$
and use $b>0$ for the last claims. Since $x^*>s_L\geq0$,
differentiability at $x^*$ gives $p'(x^*)=\gamma$. More generally,
positive selected effort implies $p'(s+e^*+a)=\gamma$ by the interior
first-order condition. At a zero-effort solution the right derivative
satisfies $p'(s+a)-\gamma\leq0$. When $s+a>x^*$ it is strictly negative:
concavity bounds $q'(s+a)$ above by the strictly negative secant slope
from $x^*$ to $s+a$. A zero derivative alone can leave multiple optima,
in which case the largest-maximizer rule determines the selection.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Stationarity and finite-horizon occupation]\label{lem:stationary}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, each fixed $a\geq0$
has a unique stationary distribution,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
 \share(a)=\frac{\lam(a)}{\lam(a)+\mu},\qquad
 \pi_L(a)=\frac{\mu}{\lam(a)+\mu}.
\end{equation}
Write $q_a=\lam(a)+\mu>0$. If $h_0\in[0,1]$ is the initial probability
of $H$, its probability at time $t\geq0$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transient}
 h(t)=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))e^{-q_a t}.
\end{equation}
For $T>0$, the occupation fraction
$Y_T=T^{-1}\int_0^T\mathbf1_{\{X_t=H\}}\,dt$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occupation}
 \mathbb E[Y_T]=\share(a)+(h_0-\share(a))
                       \frac{1-e^{-q_aT}}{q_aT}.
\end{equation}
Starting in $L$ therefore creates a downward bias of
$\share(a)(1-e^{-q_aT})/(q_aT)$ relative to stationarity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The generator in order $(L,H)$ is
\[
 Q(a)=\begin{pmatrix}-\lam(a)&\lam(a)\\ \mu&-\mu\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both off-diagonal rates are strictly positive by
Assumption~\ref{ass:chain}; the finite chain is irreducible. Stationary
balance and normalization are
$\pi_L\lam=\share\mu$ and $\pi_L+\share=1$.
They imply $(\lam+\mu)\share=\lam$. Division is valid because
$\lam\geq\lambda_0>0$ and $\mu>0$. The resulting probabilities are
positive, sum to one, and uniquely solve the two equations.
Let $X_t\in\{L,H\}$ be the state at time $t$. Finite exit rates bound
the jump intensity, so the chain is nonexplosive. In an interval of
length $d>0$ the probability of two or more jumps is $O(d^2)$. The
exponential holding times give the probabilities of changing state as
$\lam d+o(d)$ from $L$ and $\mu d+o(d)$ from $H$. Conditioning on
$X_t$, the probability of $H$ gains $(1-h(t))\lam d+o(d)$ and loses
$h(t)\mu d+o(d)$. Dividing the change by $d$ and taking the limit gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward}
 h'(t)=\lam(a)(1-h(t))-\mu h(t)=\lam(a)-q_a h(t).
\end{equation}
Subtract $\share=\lam/q_a$ to obtain
$(h-\share)'=-q_a(h-\share)$, with unique solution
\eqref{eq:transient}. Using $h_0=0$ and $h_0=1$ for the two initial
states and taking complements gives the transition matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transition}
 P_a(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \pi_L+\share e^{-q_at} & \share(1-e^{-q_at})\\
 \pi_L(1-e^{-q_at}) & \share+\pi_L e^{-q_at}
 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\pi_L$ and $\share$ are evaluated at $a$. Both rows converge
to the stationary distribution, and $h_0=\share(a)$ gives
$h(t)=\share(a)$ at every time, verifying invariance. Boundedness of the
indicator allows expectation and
integration to be interchanged. Integrating \eqref{eq:transient} on $[0,T]$
and dividing by $T$ proves \eqref{eq:occupation}. Set $h_0=0$ for the
stated bias in magnitude. The signed bias is negative from $L$, positive
from $H$, and zero when $h_0=\share$. The factor
$(1-e^{-q_aT})/(q_aT)$ tends to zero, so expected occupation converges
to stationarity.
\end{proof}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
For $\mu>0$ the map $g(\lambda)=\lambda/(\lambda+\mu)$ is strictly
increasing, with $g'(\lambda)=\mu/(\lambda+\mu)^2>0$.
Every stationary comparison therefore has the sign of its learning-rate
comparison. With $\mu=0$ and $\lam>0$, $H$ would be absorbing and its
stationary probability would be one for all assistance levels.

\begin{proposition}[Global shape, maximum, and comparison with no AI]\label{prop:shape}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, $\share$ is continuous
on $[0,\infty)$. For $0\leq a<b$, its derivative (the right derivative
at $0$) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative}
 \share'(a)=\frac{\mu\kappa[\eta(b-2a)-1]}{[\lam(a)+\mu]^2}.
\end{equation}
The following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item If $\eta b\leq1$, $\share$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,b]$,
 has its unique global maximum at $a=0$, and
 $\share(a)<\share(0)$ for all $a>0$.
 If $\eta b=1$, $\share'(0+)=0$ but $\share'(a)<0$ on $(0,b)$.
 \item If $\eta b>1$, define
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:thresholds}
 \peak=\frac{b-1/\eta}{2},\qquad \ret=b-1/\eta=2\peak.
 \end{equation}
 Then $0<\peak<\ret<b$ and $\peak<b/2$; $\share$ is strictly
 increasing on $[0,\peak]$, strictly decreasing on $[\peak,b]$, and
 has its unique global maximum at $\peak$. Moreover,
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline}
 \share(a)>\share(0)\iff0<a<\ret,\qquad
 \share(\ret)=\share(0),\qquad
 \share(a)<\share(0)\quad(a>\ret).
 \end{equation}
 \item In either regime, for all $a\geq b$, effort is zero and
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:plateau}
 \lam(a)=\lambda_0,\qquad
 \share(a)=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_0+\mu}<\share(0).
 \end{equation}
 At $b$ the one-sided derivatives are
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:kink}
 \share'(b-)=-\frac{\mu\kappa(1+\eta b)}{(\lambda_0+\mu)^2}<0,
 \qquad \share'(b+)=0.
 \end{equation}
 The share is strictly concave on $[0,b]$ and fails to be concave
 on $[0,\infty)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
On $[0,b]$ the effective-effort level is the quadratic below. Its
derivatives are taken on the active branch, using one-sided limits
at the endpoints.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
 f(a)=b+(\eta b-1)a-\eta a^2,\qquad
 f'(a)=\eta(b-2a)-1,\qquad f''(a)=-2\eta.
\end{equation}
The quotient and chain rules give \eqref{eq:derivative}; its denominator is
positive and $\mu\kappa>0$. When $\eta b\leq1$, $f'(a)<0$ for
every $a\in(0,b)$: if $\eta=0$, it equals $-1$, and otherwise
$\eta b-1-2\eta a<0$. The mean value theorem gives strict decrease,
including endpoint $0$ even when its right derivative vanishes. At $b$,
$f(a)>0=f(b)$ for each $a<b$ extends the strict comparison.

When $\eta b>1$, necessarily $\eta>0$. Solving $f'(a)=0$ gives
$\peak$. The condition $b>1/\eta$ gives $\peak>0$; $1/\eta>0$
gives $\peak<b/2$, and $0<\peak<2\peak=\ret<b$.
The derivative is positive before $\peak$ and negative after it. Strict
monotonicity of $g$ transfers these comparisons to $\share$.
For $0<a<b$, subtract $f(0)=b$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:baseline-factor}
 f(a)-b=a[\eta(b-a)-1].
\end{equation}
If $\eta b\leq1$, the bracket is negative for $a>0$. If
$\eta b>1$, it is positive exactly for $a<\ret$, zero at $\ret$,
and negative thereafter. This proves the active-region no-AI comparisons.
At $a=0$ the shares agree, so a strict gain excludes zero.

For $a\geq b$, the positive part in \eqref{eq:f} vanishes, proving
\eqref{eq:plateau}. Since $f(0)=b>0$, the plateau lies strictly below
the no-AI share. The positive part is continuous, and the rational
denominator remains positive, proving continuity at $b$.
Take $a\uparrow b$ in \eqref{eq:derivative} to obtain the left derivative
in \eqref{eq:kink}. The right derivative is zero because $\share$ is
constant on $[b,\infty)$. These signs and the plateau also establish
uniqueness of the claimed global maxima. On $(0,b)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second}
 \share''(a)=-\frac{2\mu\kappa\eta}{(\lam+\mu)^2}
             -\frac{2\mu\kappa^2[f'(a)]^2}{(\lam+\mu)^3}<0.
\end{equation}
If $\eta>0$, the first term is strictly negative; if $\eta=0$,
$f'=-1$ makes the second strictly negative. Continuity at the endpoints
and strict decrease of the interior derivative imply strict concavity
on $[0,b]$. For any $0<\varepsilon<b$,
$\share(b-\varepsilon)>\share(b)=\share(b+\varepsilon)$. Concavity
would require $\share(b)$ to be at least the average of those two
endpoint values. This midpoint violation establishes failure of global
concavity.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Comparison of two assistance levels]\label{prop:pairwise}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, for any $0\leq u<v$,
with $D_w=\lambda_0+\kappa f(w)+\mu>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:globaldiff}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=\frac{\mu\kappa[f(v)-f(u)]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
If $v\leq b$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairwise}
 \share(v)-\share(u)=
 \frac{\mu\kappa(v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1]}{D_vD_u}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\share(v)>\share(u)$ if and only if $\eta(b-u-v)>1$;
the analogous equality and strict-reverse equivalences also hold.
If $u<b\leq v$, $\share(v)<\share(u)$.
If $b\leq u<v$, the shares agree.
In two states, the share comparison also characterizes first-order stochastic
dominance and the ordering of mean skill.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the fractions in \eqref{eq:stationary}. The common numerator is
$\lam(v)[\lam(u)+\mu]-\lam(u)[\lam(v)+\mu]
=\mu[\lam(v)-\lam(u)]$, proving \eqref{eq:globaldiff}.
For $v\leq b$, the quadratic in \eqref{eq:quadratic} is valid at both
points, including $v=b$, so
\[
 f(v)-f(u)=(\eta b-1)(v-u)-\eta(v^2-u^2)
          =(v-u)[\eta(b-u-v)-1].
\]
The factors $D_uD_v$, $\mu\kappa$, and $v-u$ are positive, yielding
all sign equivalences. Active-branch equality requires $\eta>0$ and
$u+v=b-1/\eta>0$, which can occur only in the strong regime. If
$u<b\leq v$, then $f(u)>0=f(v)$. At $v=b$ the factored comparison
agrees with this strict inequality. If $b\leq u<v$, both effective
efforts equal zero. For the stochastic order, let $F_w$ be the
stationary skill CDF at assistance $w$. For $x<s_L$, both CDFs are
zero, and for $x\geq s_H$, both are one. For $s_L\leq x<s_H$,
$F_w(x)=1-\share(w)$. Thus $F_v(x)\leq F_u(x)$ for all $x$
exactly when $\share(v)\geq\share(u)$, with strict inequality
somewhere exactly when $\share(v)>\share(u)$. Expected skill is
$s_L+(s_H-s_L)\share(w)$, which gives the same weak, strict and
equality comparisons because $s_H-s_L>0$.
\end{proof}
An increase from $u$ to $v$ can improve skill even when $v$ exceeds the
maximum: in the strong regime the exact condition is $u+v<2\peak$.
A marginal comparison at $v$ instead requires $v<\peak$. The no-AI
comparison sets $u=0$ and requires $v<2\peak$. These statements answer
different questions; none says that every positive increase improves skill.

\begin{corollary}[Threshold locations and outcome magnitudes]\label{cor:sensitivity}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:worker}--\ref{ass:chain}, critical complementarity
is $\eta_c=1/b$. In the strong regime the thresholds $\peak$ and $\ret$
depend only on $\eta,b$, independently of $\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu$.
Holding the other arguments fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-sensitivity}
 \partial_\eta\peak=\frac{1}{2\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_\eta\ret=\frac{1}{\eta^2},\quad
 \partial_b\peak=\frac12,\quad \partial_b\ret=1.
\end{equation}
For fixed $a\geq0$ and $D=\lam(a)+\mu$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:level-sensitivity}
 \partial_{\lambda_0}\share=\frac{\mu}{D^2}>0,\qquad
 \partial_\kappa\share=\frac{\mu f(a)}{D^2}\geq0,\qquad
 \partial_\mu\share=-\frac{\lam(a)}{D^2}<0.
\end{equation}
The derivative in $\kappa$ is zero exactly when $a\geq b$. Also
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effort-sensitivity}
 \partial_\eta\share=\frac{\mu\kappa a(b-a)^+}{D^2}\geq0,\qquad
 \partial_b\share=\frac{\mu\kappa(1+\eta a)}{D^2}>0\quad(a<b).
\end{equation}
The $\eta$ derivative is a right derivative at $\eta=0$ and is zero
exactly when $a=0$ or $a\geq b$. The $b$ derivative is zero for $a>b$.
For fixed $a>0$ at $b=a$, its left value is zero and its right value
is $\mu\kappa(1+\eta a)/(\lambda_0+\mu)^2$.
Multiplying all three rates $(\lambda_0,\kappa,\mu)$ by the same
$c>0$ leaves the entire stationary curve unchanged and multiplies the
convergence rate $q_a$ by $c$.

For a fixed $0<a<\ret$ in the strong regime, the positive no-AI gain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
 G(a):=\share(a)-\share(0)
 =\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta}{(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)+\mu)
                             (\lambda_0+\kappa b+\mu)},\qquad
 \Delta=f(a)-b>0.
\end{equation}
It strictly decreases with $\lambda_0$. Its derivative in $\mu$ has the
sign of $(\lambda_0+\kappa f(a))(\lambda_0+\kappa b)-\mu^2$;
its derivative in $\kappa$ has the sign of
$(\lambda_0+\mu)^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b$.
The unique zero of each derivative in its positive parameter domain is
the maximum of the gain in that parameter, holding the others fixed.
At the strong-regime assistance maximum, the increase in the upward rate
and the corresponding stationary gain are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:peak-gain}
 \Delta_L=\lam(\peak)-\lam(0)=\frac{\kappa(\eta b-1)^2}{4\eta},
 \qquad
 G(\peak)=\frac{\mu\Delta_L}{[\lam(\peak)+\mu][\lam(0)+\mu]}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The regime boundary and thresholds follow from
Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}. The critical value satisfies
$d\eta_c/db=-1/b^2<0$, and differentiation of \eqref{eq:thresholds}
gives \eqref{eq:threshold-sensitivity}. Apply the quotient rule in
\eqref{eq:stationary}, using
$\partial_\kappa\lam=f$, $\partial_{\lambda_0}\lam=1$,
$\partial_\eta\lam=\kappa a(b-a)^+$, and, for $a<b$,
$\partial_b\lam=\kappa(1+\eta a)$, to obtain the level formulas.
Positivity of the parameters reduces the zero-derivative conditions
in $\kappa$ and $\eta$ to the vanishing of the corresponding
numerators. At fixed $a$, the $b$ derivative follows on either side
of the cutoff by differentiating $(b-a)^+$, then taking the limits
using continuity of $D$.
For common scaling, cancel $c$ in $c\lam/(c\lam+c\mu)$; the sum of the
rates becomes $cq_a$, proving the convergence statement using
Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}.
Setting $u=0$ in \eqref{eq:globaldiff} proves \eqref{eq:gain}.
Let $C_1=\lambda_0+\kappa f(a)>0$, $C_0=\lambda_0+\kappa b>0$,
$M=(C_1+\mu)(C_0+\mu)>0$, and $B=\lambda_0+\mu>0$.
Holding $a,b,\eta$ fixed, differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \partial_{\lambda_0}G
 &=-\frac{\mu\kappa\Delta[(C_1+\mu)+(C_0+\mu)]}{M^2}<0,
 \label{eq:gain-lambda}\\
 \partial_\mu G&=\frac{\kappa\Delta(C_1C_0-\mu^2)}{M^2},
 \label{eq:gain-mu}\\
 \partial_\kappa G&=\frac{\mu\Delta[B^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b]}{M^2}.
 \label{eq:gain-kappa}
\end{align}
For the second formula, the quotient numerator after pulling out
$\kappa\Delta$ is $M-\mu\,\partial_\mu M=C_1C_0-\mu^2$.
For the third, expand $M=B^2+B\kappa(f(a)+b)+\kappa^2 f(a)b$,
so $M-\kappa\,\partial_\kappa M=B^2-\kappa^2 f(a)b$.
The denominators and remaining factors are positive. The signs change
from positive to negative at $\mu=\sqrt{C_1C_0}$ and
$\kappa=B/\sqrt{f(a)b}$, respectively. Since both roots are positive,
each gives the unique maximum in its parameter on $(0,\infty)$.
Finally, writing $d=\eta b-1>0$, \eqref{eq:quadratic} implies
$f(d/(2\eta))-b=d^2/(4\eta)$. Multiplication by $\kappa$ and
substitution into \eqref{eq:gain} yield \eqref{eq:peak-gain}.
\end{proof}
Threshold invariance holds conditional on fixed $b,\eta$, not on arbitrary
changes to the worker problem. For the numerical example
$p(x)=A\log(1+x)$, $A>\gamma>0$, one has
$x^*=A/\gamma-1$ and $b=A/\gamma-1-s_L>0$.
Raising $A$ increases $b$; raising $\gamma$ or $s_L$ lowers it.
With $b$ and the rates fixed, \eqref{eq:peak-gain} makes the stationary
gain second order in $\eta b-1$ near the critical value. Indeed,
$\eta\to1/b>0$ and $\lam(\peak)\to\lam(0)$ at that boundary, so the
coefficient on $(\eta b-1)^2$ tends to a finite positive constant.
The stationary curve depends on the dimensionless arguments
$a/b$, $\eta b$, $\lambda_0/\mu$, and $\kappa b/\mu$. Factoring $b$
from $f$ gives $f(a)=b(1-a/b)^+[1+(\eta b)(a/b)]$.
Dividing the numerator and denominator of \eqref{eq:stationary} by
$\mu$ then gives the claimed dependence.

\section{Simulations and numerical checks}\label{sec:sim}
\subsection{Design and economic interpretation}
All parameters are illustrative, not estimated or calibrated to data.
Use $p(x)=4\log(1+x)$, $\gamma=1$, $s_L=1$, and $s_H=3$;
then $x^*=3$ and $b=2$. Set $\lambda_0=0.1$, $\kappa=0.4$, and
$\mu=0.3$, with rates per chosen time unit. Without measured effort the
mean upward waiting time is ten units; the mean high-state spell is
$1/0.3$ units. These units have no assigned calendar interpretation.
Assistance and effort have the same production-input units.

We compare $\eta\in\{0,0.25,1\}$, so
$\eta b\in\{0,0.5,2\}$. The first regime excludes complementarity;
the second allows it below the critical value; the third crosses the
condition in Proposition~\ref{prop:shape}. A separate equality check uses
$\eta=0.5$ to test the zero right derivative at $a=0$ and the strict
decline at positive assistance in the active region.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../code/figures/mechanism.pdf}
 \caption{Formula evaluations of effort, learning, and stationary skill.
 Effort is identical across regimes. Only strong complementarity makes the

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} separates behavior from learning. Effort falls
one-for-one until $a=2$ in every regime: the learning technology does not
alter that response. Without complementarity learning falls immediately.
Weak complementarity raises skill at a given active assistance level
relative to $\eta=0$ and leaves the assistance profile decreasing. Strong
complementarity raises learning for $0<a<0.5$ even as effort falls.
Beyond the maximum, additional effectiveness no longer offsets displaced
effort. Every regime meets the same zero-effort plateau.

In the strong example, the maximum is at $a=0.5$, the return to the no-AI
share at $a=1$, and effort disappears at $a=2$. The no-AI share is $3/4$;
the maximum is $10/13$, a gain of $1/52$, approximately \PeakGainPP{}
percentage points. The plateau is $1/4$. Indeed, $f(0)=2$,
$f(0.5)=2.25$, $\lam(0)=0.9$, and $\lam(0.5)=1.0$.
Between $0.5$ and $1$, assistance lowers skill at the margin while the
share remains above its no-AI value.

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} separates location from magnitude. Baseline

why the gain need not be monotone in $\kappa$ or $\mu$.

\subsection{Continuous-time Markov simulations}
Each path draws an exponential holding time with its current state's exit
rate, then switches to the other state. There is no discrete-time transition
approximation. Occupation is time spent in $H$, including the final censored
holding spell, divided by the horizon. Counting the proportion of visits to
$H$ would be inappropriate: the embedded jump chain alternates and does not
have the same occupation law.

For each of \MCCells{} assistance/regime cells, independent paths start in $L$.
There are \MCReplications{} replications per cell and horizons
$T=25,100,400$. The cells combine the three complementarity values with
$a\in\{0,0.25,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}$. The master seed is \MCSeed{};
independent child streams make the experiment reproducible. No burn-in is
hidden. Each path is evaluated at all three horizons, so estimates at
different horizons share a trajectory; errors at a given horizon are computed
across independent paths. Means are compared with the stationary share and with the exact
finite-horizon expectation from Lemma~\ref{lem:stationary}. The standard
error is the sample standard deviation of independent path occupation
fractions divided by the square root of the replication count. Plotted
$95\%$ intervals use this replication-level error, rather than treating
autocorrelated within-path observations as independent.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=.94\linewidth]{../code/figures/ctmc.pdf}
 \caption{Stochastic occupation estimates and analytical predictions.
 Points and Monte Carlo intervals use independent continuous-time paths.
 The finite-horizon mean accounts for starting in $L$; stationary values are
 limiting predictions. Their gap can reflect both Monte Carlo error and
 initial-condition bias.}\label{fig:ctmc}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ctmc} checks whether simulated dynamics reproduce the
analytical occupation target. The exact finite-horizon means isolate
the effect of the initial state. For the mean $\overline Y_T$ of the
simulated occupations in a cell,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mc-decomposition}
 \overline Y_T-\share
 =\underbrace{\overline Y_T-\mathbb E[Y_T]}_{\text{Monte Carlo error}}
 +\underbrace{\mathbb E[Y_T]-\share}_{\text{initial-state bias}}.
\end{equation}
At $T=\MCHorizon$, the maximum absolute
gap from stationarity across the simulated cells is \MCMaxAbsErrorPP{}
percentage points, and the largest gap from the finite-horizon mean is
\MCMaxFiniteErrorPP{} percentage points. The largest absolute exact
initial-state bias at that horizon is \MCMaxBiasPP{} percentage points.
The largest standardized
finite-horizon error across the checked horizons is \MCMaxZ{} exact
standard errors. These are realized implementation diagnostics, not an
estimate of an economic effect. Exact formula evaluation illustrates a
proven comparative static; the stochastic experiment checks the predicted
law of the chain. Neither substitutes for the proof, and finitely many
paths cannot by themselves establish a long-run limit.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The marginal learning effect decomposes as follows for $a<b$.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mechanism}
 \lam'(a)=\underbrace{\kappa\eta(b-a)}_{\text{effectiveness of retained effort}}
          -\underbrace{\kappa(1+\eta a)}_{\text{displaced effort}}.
\end{equation}
Additional assistance makes the remaining effort more productive for
learning. At the same time, it removes one unit of effort whose learning
value rises with assistance. The first term therefore falls with
assistance while the second grows. A larger $b$ puts more effort at the
disposal of the complementary technology and reduces the critical
complementarity $1/b$. The active region is essential. If the low-skilled
worker begins at a zero-effort corner ($b\leq0$), \eqref{eq:effort}
gives zero effort for every feasible $a$, so only baseline learning
remains. The common positive depreciation rate makes a higher learning
rate matter for the stationary allocation of time across states.

The separation of effort and learning comes from myopia. The worker
responds to current output and effort cost, while the learning rate
changes the worker's subsequent skill. A worker who values future skill
could respond to changed learning returns when choosing effort itself,
so the one-for-one displacement calculation would need to be revisited.
The bilinear specification also makes the productivity effect proportional
to remaining effort and imposes a constant cross-partial. The observed
thresholds and curvature depend on that specification. Different
learning responses as effort approaches zero could change the shape
near the cutoff.

The two-state restriction converts the stationary distribution into one
probability. The high-skill level affects mean skill and current
production, while only the low state's effort governs upward
transitions. With additional states, distinct effort cutoffs could make
the upward rates move in different directions, and a single probability
would no longer order the distribution. Fixed assistance also makes the
generator time-homogeneous. Assistance that responded to current skill
or changed over time would alter the transition process and the
interpretation of a stationary comparison.

Stationary skill maximization is distinct from productivity and
welfare. By \eqref{eq:effort}, stationary expected gross output at
constant assistance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:output}
 \sum_{i\in\{L,H\}}\pi_i(a)\,
    p\bigl(\max\{x^*,s_i+a\}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Assistance changes both the weights and the output produced conditional
on each skill. For the unbounded logarithmic production function in
Section~\ref{sec:sim}, the output in \eqref{eq:output} is bounded
below by $p(a)$ and grows without bound while the skill share is
constant for $a\geq b$. Thus even under strong complementarity the
assistance level that maximizes stationary skill can yield less gross
output than a higher assistance level. A welfare comparison would also
have to specify the resource cost of AI, account for effort costs, and
value skill and production during the transition as well as in
stationarity.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In a two-state model with myopic effort, AI can raise the learning
productivity of remaining effort enough to reverse stationary skill
degradation. Reversal holds exactly when $\eta b>1$. In that case
$\share$ increases up to $\peak$ and remains above the no-AI
benchmark until $\ret=2\peak$. The pairwise comparison specifies when
an assistance increase helps even if its endpoint exceeds the peak.
At $b$, the worker stops supplying effort and the skill share falls to
the plateau determined by baseline learning and depreciation. The
magnitude of the gain depends on the transition rates, while the
assistance thresholds are governed by no-AI effort and learning
complementarity.

\section{Occupation variance for the simulation diagnostic}\label{app:mc}
Fix assistance and abbreviate $q=q_a$, $\pi=\share(a)$, and
$J_t=\mathbf1_{\{X_t=H\}}$. With $X_0=L$ and $0\leq s\leq t$,
the Markov property and \eqref{eq:transition} give
\[
 \mathbb E[J_sJ_t]=\pi(1-e^{-qs})[\pi+(1-\pi)e^{-q(t-s)}].
\]
Squaring the integral defining $Y_T$ and using boundedness allows
expectation to enter the double integral. The integrand is symmetric
in the two times and the diagonal has measure zero. Integrating first
in $t$ over $s\leq t\leq T$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occupation-second}
 \mathbb E[Y_T^2]
 =\frac{2\pi}{T^2}\left[\pi I_1+\frac{1-\pi}{q} I_2\right],
 \quad
 \begin{aligned}
 I_1&=\int_0^T(1-e^{-qs})(T-s)\,ds,\\
 I_2&=\int_0^T(1-e^{-qs})(1-e^{-q(T-s)})\,ds .
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Writing $d_T=1-e^{-qT}$, integration by parts gives
$\int_0^T e^{-qs}(T-s)\,ds=T/q-d_T/q^2$. Expansion of the
product in $I_2$ gives two single-exponential terms whose integrals
are both $d_T/q$ and a constant cross term $e^{-qT}$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:occupation-variance}
 I_1=\frac{T^2}{2}-\frac{T}{q}+\frac{d_T}{q^2},
 \qquad I_2=T(1+e^{-qT})-\frac{2d_T}{q},
 \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_T)=\mathbb E[Y_T^2]-\mathbb E[Y_T]^2.
\end{equation}
For $N$ independent paths at the same horizon, the exact standard error
is $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Y_T)/N}$. It differs from the empirical
standard error used to plot the confidence intervals.
